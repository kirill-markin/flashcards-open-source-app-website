“The gate opened, we stepped inside” has a comma splice. Remove the comma and you get a fused sentence: “The gate opened we stepped inside.” Both are short. Their problem is how two complete clauses meet.

The difference between a **comma splice and a fused sentence** is the punctuation at that boundary. First find the clauses, then choose a repair that keeps the writer's meaning. The examples and answers below follow standard academic English punctuation for school writing and grammar practice.

## Two errors at the same boundary

| Type | What joins the independent clauses? | Original example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comma splice | A comma without a coordinating conjunction | The gate opened, we stepped inside. |
| Fused sentence | No punctuation or conjunction | The gate opened we stepped inside. |
| Correct compound sentence | A comma and a coordinating conjunction | The gate opened, and we stepped inside. |

Both errors are commonly grouped under **run-on sentences**, as [Michigan's Sweetland Center for Writing explains](https://lsa.umich.edu/sweetland/undergraduates/writing-guides/what-is-a-run-on-sentence---how-do-i-fix-it-.html). A long sentence can be correct; a five-word sentence can contain a faulty join.

## Find what can stand alone

An **independent clause** can stand as a complete sentence. In the statements here, it has a subject and a verb and makes a complete statement: “The printer stopped.” A **dependent clause** also has a subject and a verb, but it can't stand alone: “When the printer stopped.” The word *when* makes that statement depend on something else.

Use this check before changing punctuation:

- Find the subject and what is said about it. A verb can describe an action (*stopped*) or a state (*was* in “the battery was empty”).
- Test each possible clause as a sentence, keeping words such as *when, although, because,* and *if* attached. Removing those words changes the test.
- If both clauses can stand alone, inspect what joins them. A comma alone gives a comma splice; no punctuation or joining word gives a fused sentence.

[George Mason's writing-center guide](https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/writing-resources/grammar-style/run-on-sentences) uses this approach: locate the subjects and verbs, find the boundaries, then revise the join.

Try this on “The printer stopped the queue grew.” You can divide it into:

- **The printer stopped.** Subject: *the printer*; verb: *stopped*.
- **The queue grew.** Subject: *the queue*; verb: *grew*.

Both work alone. Their unmarked boundary makes the original a fused sentence.

Now compare “When the printer stopped, the queue grew.” “When the printer stopped” leaves you waiting for what happened; “the queue grew” answers that question. The first clause is dependent, so this comma doesn't splice two independent clauses. It separates an introductory dependent clause from the main clause, following the pattern in [Purdue OWL's comma guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/commas/extended_rules_for_commas.html).

Two verbs don't necessarily mean two clauses. Compare these sentences:

- **Maya checked the address and sent the letter.** One subject, *Maya*, shares two linked verb phrases. This is a **compound predicate** within one clause; no comma is needed before *and*.
- **Maya checked the address, and she sent the letter.** “Maya checked the address” and “she sent the letter” each have a subject and can stand alone. The comma plus *and* joins two independent clauses.

Both sentences describe the same person doing the same things. The clause structure changes when the second statement gets its own subject, *she*. The [Naval Postgraduate School's comma guide](https://nps.edu/web/gwc/commas-fanboys) explains why conjunctions joining two verb phrases work differently from those joining two independent clauses.

## Repair the join without changing the story

For two related independent clauses, these repairs work:

- **Period:** The gate opened. We stepped inside.
- **Semicolon:** The gate opened; we stepped inside.
- **Comma plus coordinating conjunction:** The gate opened, and we stepped inside.

The coordinating conjunctions are *for, and, nor, but, or, yet,* and *so*—often remembered as **FANBOYS**. Choose one for its meaning: *and* adds information, *but* signals contrast, and *so* presents a result.

Making a clause dependent also works. “Because the gate opened, we stepped inside” explicitly gives a reason; “When the gate opened, we stepped inside” specifies timing. A period or semicolon leaves the relationship less explicit. If the task only asks you to fix the punctuation, don't add a cause the sentence hasn't established.

## Why however doesn't fix a comma splice

**However** and **therefore** don't work like *and* or *but* at this boundary. For example:

- Incorrect: The route was longer, however, it was safer.
- Correct: The route was longer; however, it was safer.
- Also correct: The route was longer. However, it was safer.

The comma after *however* doesn't fix the comma splice before it. Another repair is “The route was longer, but it was safer.” Here, *but* is a coordinating conjunction, so a comma works before it.

The same semicolon pattern works with *therefore*: “The road was closed; therefore, we turned back.” *However* expresses contrast and *therefore* expresses a result, but neither provides the grammatical join that *but* or *so* does. [Purdue OWL's clause guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html) explains this distinction.

## Diagnose nine sentences

Label each sentence **comma splice**, **fused sentence**, or **correct**. For each error, mark the boundary and write one correction. For each correct sentence, explain why its join works. Try these original exercises before reading the answers.

1. The museum closes at six, our bus arrives at seven.
2. Lena packed her notebook and checked the forecast.
3. Although the room was crowded, everyone found a seat.
4. The timer rang the bread was ready.
5. The instructions looked simple, however, one step was missing.
6. We saved the draft, and the editor reviewed it.
7. The battery was empty; therefore, the recorder stopped.
8. We stayed indoors because the wind was strong.
9. Omar found the receipt he requested a refund.

### Answers with the clause test

1. **Comma splice**, between *six* and *our*. “The museum closes at six” and “our bus arrives at seven” can each stand alone. One repair: “The museum closes at six; our bus arrives at seven.” A period also works; adding *but* would explicitly frame the times as a conflict.
2. **Correct.** *Lena* is the shared subject of *packed* and *checked*. The second verb phrase doesn't have a separate subject here; the two actions form one compound predicate. Leave the sentence as it is.
3. **Correct.** “Although the room was crowded” is dependent. The comma follows that introductory clause; it doesn't join two independent ones.
4. **Fused sentence**, between *rang* and *the*. “The timer rang” and “the bread was ready” are independent clauses. Repair: “The timer rang. The bread was ready.” This reports both facts without claiming the ringing caused the bread to be ready.
5. **Comma splice**, before *however*. Repair: “The instructions looked simple; however, one step was missing.” Both clauses are independent, and *however* signals contrast without acting as a coordinating conjunction.
6. **Correct.** “We saved the draft” and “the editor reviewed it” are independent clauses, joined by a comma plus *and*.
7. **Correct.** “The battery was empty” and “the recorder stopped” are independent clauses separated by a semicolon. *Therefore*, followed by a comma, introduces the stated result.
8. **Correct.** “We stayed indoors” is independent; “because the wind was strong” is dependent. This main-clause-first sentence doesn't need a comma before *because*.
9. **Fused sentence**, between *receipt* and *he*. “Omar found the receipt” and “he requested a refund” can each stand alone, even though *Omar* and *he* refer to the same person. One repair: “Omar found the receipt, and he requested a refund.”

## Choose a repair for the intended meaning

For each item, identify the error and rewrite the sentence to match the instruction. Several answers can work; the explanations below show what to preserve.

10. “Asha studies biology, Ben studies history.” Report two facts without adding a reason or contrast.
11. “The door was locked we used the side entrance.” Make the second action a result of the first fact.
12. “The bag was small, it held all the equipment.” Emphasize the unexpected contrast.

### Worked repairs

10. **Comma splice:** “Asha studies biology. Ben studies history.” A semicolon or a comma plus *and* also works. “Asha studies biology because Ben studies history” adds a causal claim the prompt never supplied.
11. **Fused sentence:** “The door was locked, so we used the side entrance.” Another answer is “Because the door was locked, we used the side entrance.” A period would repair the grammar, but *so* or *because* makes the requested reason-and-result relationship explicit.
12. **Comma splice:** “The bag was small, but it held all the equipment.” “Although the bag was small, it held all the equipment” also works. “The bag was small; however, it held all the equipment” preserves the contrast too. Adding *so* would present capacity as a result of small size, which doesn't fit the intended meaning.

## Try the rule on a fresh sentence

**“The ferry was delayed, the passengers waited under the shelter.”** Identify the join, repair it without adding a cause, then write a second version that explicitly presents the waiting as a result of the delay. Explain the difference before checking below.

The original is a **comma splice**: both clauses can stand alone. A neutral repair is “The ferry was delayed. The passengers waited under the shelter.” A semicolon also works. For the result version, write “The ferry was delayed, so the passengers waited under the shelter.” The first version reports two related facts; *so* explicitly says the delay led to the waiting.
