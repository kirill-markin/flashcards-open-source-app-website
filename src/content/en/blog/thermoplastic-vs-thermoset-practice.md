A cured epoxy part can lose stiffness when heated even though it won't melt into a liquid you can mold again. That distinction matters in a selection question: “thermoset” describes a cured network, not a promise that a part will survive any temperature.

The basic **thermoplastic vs thermoset** distinction is reversible thermal softening versus chemical curing. To use it in a materials question, separate how the part takes shape from what it must do in service. The seven original questions below cover classification, recycling assumptions, and a worked selection worksheet.

## Start with what happens to the chains

In the conventional examples used here, a **thermoplastic** softens on heating and hardens on cooling reversibly. Its chains can move relative to one another during suitable thermal processing.

A **thermosetting resin** cures through a chemical reaction that links chains into a network; the cured material is a **thermoset**. These chemical crosslinks prevent ordinary remelting. [UNSW's polymer tutorial](https://www.unsw.edu.au/science/our-schools/materials/engage-with-us/high-school-students-and-teachers/online-tutorials/polymers/structure-and-form/polymer-types) explains the chain structures and curing process.

| Question | Thermoplastic | Conventional cured thermoset |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What fixes the shape in these processes? | Cooling after thermal shaping | Formation of a crosslinked network during cure |
| Can heating enable reshaping? | Yes, within a suitable processing window | Heating doesn't restore a moldable melt |
| What should a processing answer mention? | Heating, shaping, and cooling | Shaping the resin system and completing its cure |
| Does the family name give a service-temperature limit? | No | No |

Reversible softening doesn't promise unlimited processing cycles without damage. The [Open University's introduction to polymers](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/chemistry/introduction-polymers/content-section-1.2.2) notes that heat and oxidation can degrade thermoplastics during reprocessing. A suitable processing window matters.

## Examples worth telling apart

The Open University course above gives these common examples. They identify polymer families; they don't specify the performance of a commercial grade.

| Thermoplastics | Thermosets, after curing |
| --- | --- |
| Polyethylene: HDPE and LDPE | Epoxy resin |
| PET: polyethylene terephthalate | Unsaturated polyester resin |
| Nylon: polyamide | Phenol-formaldehyde resin, often called phenolic |
| PMMA: acrylic | Melamine-formaldehyde resin |

Notice the two polyesters. **PET is a thermoplastic; a conventional cured unsaturated polyester resin is a thermoset.** “Polyester” alone leaves the classification incomplete. Appearance is also a poor shortcut: a rigid acrylic part is still thermoplastic.

### Softening, melting, and failure are different events

The **glass transition, Tg**, is associated with increased chain mobility in amorphous regions as a polymer warms, and a change in stiffness. It can occur over a range of temperatures. It isn't a melting point. A semicrystalline thermoplastic has crystalline regions as well; their melting is a separate transition. The Open University treats [glass transition](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/chemistry/introduction-polymers/content-section-2.5.3) and [melting](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/chemistry/introduction-polymers/content-section-2.5.4) separately.

A cured thermoset can soften and lose useful stiffness while its network remains connected. More severe heating can cause chemical degradation, rather than a reusable melt. [CKN's thermoset overview](https://compositeskn.org/KPC/A105) describes this softening followed by breakdown. So “cannot remelt” doesn't mean “cannot soften” or “heatproof.”

For a selection problem, ask for properties under the required service conditions. [CKN's material-selection guidance](https://compositeskn.org/KPC/P153) considers operating temperature, mechanical properties, and manufacturing conditions separately. Neither Tg nor the family name supplies every answer.

## Four quick classification questions

Try these before reading the answers. Assume conventional grades and processes throughout.

1. PET feedstock is heated, shaped, and cooled. Is the shape fixed primarily by cooling or by creating a cured network?
2. A two-part epoxy system reacts to form a cured crosslinked adhesive. Can the cured adhesive be reshaped by simply remelting it like PET?
3. A sample is hard and rigid at room temperature. Is that enough to call it a thermoset?
4. A PET item can, in principle, be reprocessed by melting. Does that establish that a particular collection service accepts it?

### Answers

1. **Cooling.** This is thermal shaping of a thermoplastic. No chemical cure is specified.
2. **No.** The stated reaction creates a thermoset network. Reheating doesn't reverse that cure to give a moldable melt, even if the adhesive softens.
3. **Insufficient information.** PMMA provides a counterexample: a rigid thermoplastic. Ask for the material identity or relevant processing information.
4. **No.** Ability to reprocess a polymer doesn't establish collection acceptance for an item. The [US EPA's recycling guidance](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/how-do-i-recycle-common-recyclables) says to check the local program; even a resin identification symbol doesn't establish acceptance.

## Screen candidates against a requirement sheet

Polymer material selection starts with requirements. [ATU's plastics materials selection syllabus](https://amm.atusligo.ie/en/module/web/20/MATL06018/201900/999999) connects material classification, technical data sheets, requirement specifications, and selection matrices. The worksheet below practices using those pieces together.

**Every candidate and number below is fictional classroom data.** They aren't supplier data or measured test results. Treat the values as exact and comparable under the stated conditions, including after the required reshaping. A missing value means information is needed.

A small positioning guide must meet all three requirements:

- Operate continuously at **80°C**, so the supplied maximum continuous-use limit must be **at least 80°C**.
- Have a tensile modulus at 80°C of **at least 1.2 GPa**. Tensile modulus describes material stiffness; GPa means gigapascals. The exercise supplies this threshold rather than asking you to derive it from the part's geometry.
- Allow the already formed guide to be **thermally reshaped once**. Assume suitable equipment and a compatible procedure are available wherever the table says “yes.” Replacing the part or machining it doesn't meet this requirement.

| Fictional candidate | Family | Maximum continuous-use limit | Tensile modulus at 80°C | Compatible thermal reshaping after forming? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Thermoplastic | 110°C | 1.4 GPa | Yes |
| B | Thermoplastic | 100°C | 0.9 GPa | Yes |
| C | Cured thermoset | 140°C | 2.6 GPa | No |
| D | Thermoplastic | 130°C | Not supplied | Yes |

Check all three requirements for each candidate before reading the worked answers:

5. Which candidates pass, fail, or need more information? State the controlling requirement.
6. A student chooses C because it has the highest temperature limit and stiffness. Explain the error.
7. The required operating temperature rises to 120°C, and the modulus must now be at least 1.2 GPa **at 120°C**. The reshaping requirement remains. Can any candidate pass from these data?

### Worked screening answers

**5. Only A passes this screen.** Check every column:

| Candidate | Decision | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Pass | 110°C ≥ 80°C; 1.4 GPa ≥ 1.2 GPa; reshaping is compatible |
| B | Fail | 100°C ≥ 80°C, but 0.9 GPa < 1.2 GPa; stiffness rules it out |
| C | Fail | 140°C ≥ 80°C and 2.6 GPa ≥ 1.2 GPa, but reshaping is incompatible |
| D | More information needed | 130°C ≥ 80°C and reshaping is compatible; modulus at 80°C is unknown |

Missing data don't count as a pass or a demonstrated property failure. Request D's comparable modulus data at 80°C. A higher continuous-use limit doesn't tell you its stiffness at that temperature.

**6. C fails a mandatory process requirement.** Extra stiffness cannot compensate for an incompatible process when every requirement is compulsory. If reshaping were removed from the specification, C would pass the remaining two classroom thresholds. That would be a changed problem.

**7. No candidate is demonstrated to pass.** A and B fail the new temperature requirement: 110°C < 120°C and 100°C < 120°C. C's temperature limit is sufficient, but its reshaping incompatibility still rules it out. D meets the temperature and process requirements, but needs modulus data at 120°C.

The supplied 80°C modulus values don't establish stiffness at 120°C for any candidate. Service-temperature and modulus checks must use the same required operating temperature. A or B would remain excluded even if new modulus data were supplied; C would remain excluded by its process mismatch.

Passing this worksheet is a shortlist decision. A real part also needs suitable grade-specific evidence for its geometry, loads, duration, environment, processing history, and other applicable requirements. The fictional limits aren't material approval.

## Keep the controlling requirement in your answer

When reviewing a missed problem, write the requirement beside the decision: “C fails because the part must be reshaped after forming.” This records why the answer holds. If the requirements change, screen the candidates again.
