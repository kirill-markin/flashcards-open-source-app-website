*Puella in villa currit* and *Puella in villam currit* both describe a girl running. In the first, she runs **inside the country house**; in the second, she runs **into it**. The verb stays the same. The noun ending changes the relationship.

Short sentences let you practise two things that a vocabulary gloss leaves out: the case a preposition requires, and the relationship a connector expresses. Knowing that *cum* can mean “with” or “when” helps only if you can tell which construction you are reading.

This Latin prepositions and conjunctions practice assumes you can read simple sentences and recognise basic noun and verb forms, including some subjunctives. English explanations and German meaning cues accompany the examples. The ten exercises and complete answers work on their own; the optional companion deck uses **German prompts and explanations with Latin examples**.

## Separate the case question from the meaning question

A preposition's **case government** is the case it requires from the noun or pronoun that goes with it. In German study notes, this is *Kasusrektion*. Learn that requirement separately from the translation.

For *apud*, the case question is “Which Latin case?” The answer is **accusative**. In *Discipulus apud magistrum manet*, the meaning question asks what *apud magistrum* expresses: “with the teacher,” or German *beim Lehrer*. The German dative in *beim Lehrer* doesn't change the Latin accusative *magistrum*.

Use the same separation when making flashcards:

| Task | Front | Answer first, then explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recall government | Which case does *apud* require? | **Accusative.** Example: *apud magistrum*. |
| Interpret a phrase | What does *apud magistrum* mean in *Discipulus apud magistrum manet*? | **With the teacher / beim Lehrer.** The pupil stays with the teacher. |

Grade the answer against the task. A meaning card can accept a natural English or German equivalent. A government card needs the Latin case: translating *apud magistrum* correctly doesn't show that you can name its case.

## Use location and destination carefully

For the spatial uses of *in* and *sub*, distinguish **where something happens** from **where something goes**. [Allen and Greenough's preposition reference, §§220–221](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/uses-prepositions), gives the case distinction and the requirements for the other prepositions below.

| Latin sentence | Meaning | Case and German cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Puella in villa currit.* | The girl runs inside the country house. | Ablative *villa*: **wo? — im Landhaus** |
| *Puella in villam currit.* | The girl runs into the country house. | Accusative *villam*: **wohin? — ins Landhaus hinein** |
| *Canis sub plaustro dormit.* | The dog sleeps under the wagon. | Ablative *plaustro*: **wo? — unter dem Wagen** |
| *Canis sub plaustrum currit.* | The dog runs under the wagon. | Accusative *plaustrum*: **wohin? — unter den Wagen** |

In the first sentence, the girl is already inside the house and runs there. In the second, she runs into it. The movement verb alone therefore cannot choose the case. The dog examples make the same location/destination distinction with *sub*. These spatial uses don't cover every figurative or idiomatic use of either preposition.

Learn other prepositions with their own case requirements: *apud* + accusative, *sine* + ablative, *de* + ablative. German *wo?* and *wohin?* help interpret the spatial contrast above; they don't determine the case of every Latin preposition. Someone can stay in one place after *apud*, and it still takes the accusative.

Knowing the case is one task; producing or recognising its form is another. With vowel-length marks omitted, *villa* can be nominative or ablative singular; *villam* is accusative singular. The neuter noun *plaustrum* has the same form in the nominative and accusative singular; its ablative singular is *plaustro*. The [first-declension reference](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/1st-declension-stem-paradigm-and-gender) and [second-declension reference](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/2nd-declension-stem-paradigm-and-gender) provide the paradigms. [PONS's entry for *villa*](https://de.pons.com/%C3%BCbersetzung/latein-deutsch/villa) also records the meaning *Landhaus*.

## Read the construction before translating the connector

Compare these two original sentences:

- *Lucia cum Marco laborat.* — Lucia works **with Marcus**. German: *Lucia arbeitet mit Marcus.* Here *cum* is a **preposition**, followed by ablative *Marco*.
- *Cum Marcus laborat, Lucia legit.* — **When Marcus works**, Lucia reads. German: *Wenn Marcus arbeitet, liest Lucia.* Here *cum* introduces a **subordinate clause**, with subject *Marcus* and finite verb *laborat*.

In the second example, *cum* is a **subordinating conjunction**, also called a **subordinator**. German teaching materials often use **Subjunktion** for this role. It is still a type of conjunction in the traditional classification: the distinction is between **coordination**, joining constructions of equal grammatical rank, and **subordination**, making one clause depend on another. [Allen and Greenough, §223](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/conjunctions), sets out those two classes.

Now compare two ways to give a reason:

| Latin | Meaning | Construction and German cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Marcus tacet, nam puer dormit.* | Marcus is quiet, for the boy is asleep. | *Nam* coordinates two clauses. *Marcus schweigt, **denn** der Junge schläft.* |
| *Marcus tacet, quia puer dormit.* | Marcus is quiet because the boy is asleep. | *Quia* introduces a causal subordinate clause. *Marcus schweigt, **weil** der Junge schläft.* |

Both give a reason for Marcus's silence. Recognising that meaning doesn't yet tell you the structure: *nam* coordinates, while *quia* subordinates. *Et* (“and,” *und*) and *sed* (“but,” *aber*) are coordinating conjunctions too.

In the *quia* example, *dormit* is indicative: the reason is presented as the speaker's own fact. Other contexts can require a different mood; see the [causal-clause reference, §§539–540](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/causal-clauses). A short vocabulary gloss cannot replace that context.

## Distinguish purpose, result and comparison with ut

*Ut* is especially easy to overlearn as German *damit*. Compare three uses:

| Original example | Meaning and German cue | What supports the reading? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Mater cibum parat, ut pueri cenent.* | The mother prepares food **so that the children can dine**. **damit** | Dining is the intended purpose; *cenent* is subjunctive. |
| *Nauta tam clare clamat, ut amici eum audiant.* | The sailor shouts **so loudly that his friends hear him**. **sodass** | *Tam* marks the degree; the clause gives the result. *Audiant* is subjunctive. |
| *Ut Marcus laborat, ita Lucia laborat.* | **As Marcus works, so Lucia works.** **wie … so** | *Ut … ita* makes a comparison; *laborat* is indicative. |

The [purpose-clause chapter, §531](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/purpose-clauses), and [result-clause chapter, §537](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/result-clauses), explain the first two constructions. [The conjunction reference, §323](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/uses-conjunctions), covers comparison. The [verb-formation reference, §179](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/forms-conjugation), supports the present subjunctive forms.

Both purpose and result use *ut* with a subjunctive in these examples. The mood alone therefore cannot distinguish them. The mother prepares food **with the aim** that the children eat; the sailor shouts so loudly that his friends hear him **as a consequence**. Read the relationship between the clauses. *Tam* supports the result reading here, but one cue is not a rule for every sentence.

Negative purpose commonly uses *ne*: *Nauta lente ambulat, ne puerum excitet.* — “The sailor walks slowly **so as not to wake the boy**,” German *damit er den Jungen nicht weckt*. Here *excitet* is subjunctive. This is a purpose example, not a rule for every use of *ne*.

*Cum* also has several clause uses. Its temporal reading above doesn't make every *cum* mean “when”; causal and concessive uses need their own contexts. The [cum-clause chapter, §§544–549](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/latin/cum-clauses), is a useful next reference when those appear in your reading.

## Try ten prompts before opening the answer key

For a **case** question, name the Latin case or supply the requested Latin form. For **meaning**, give an English or German equivalent of the marked phrase. For **function**, identify the construction and explain its role. All examples omit vowel-length marks.

1. **Case:** Which case follows *apud* in *Discipula apud poetam manet*?
2. **Meaning:** Translate **in villa** in *Lucia in villa currit*.
3. **Form:** Complete “Lucia runs into the country house”: *Lucia in ___ currit.* Use *villa*.
4. **Meaning:** Translate **sub plaustro** in *Puer sub plaustro sedet*.
5. **Meaning:** The old man is describing an island to his listeners. Translate **de insula** in *Senex de insula narrat*.
6. **Function:** Is *cum* a preposition or subordinator in *Marcus cum Lucia cenat*? Explain the phrase.
7. **Function:** Is *cum* a preposition or subordinator in *Cum Lucia cenat, Marcus legit*? Give its meaning here.
8. **Structure:** Compare *Marcus tacet, nam puer dormit* and *Marcus tacet, quia puer dormit*. Which connector coordinates, and which introduces a subordinate clause?
9. **Function:** Interpret *ut pueri cenent* in *Mater cibum parat, ut pueri cenent*. Purpose or result?
10. **Function:** Interpret *ut amici eum audiant* in *Nauta tam clare clamat, ut amici eum audiant*. Purpose or result?

### Answers with reasons

1. **Accusative.** *Poetam* is accusative singular: “The female pupil stays with the poet,” German *Die Schülerin bleibt beim Dichter*. The stationary situation doesn't change *apud*'s government.
2. **Inside the country house / im Landhaus.** *Villa* is ablative here. The running takes place within it.
3. **Villam:** *Lucia in villam currit.* The house is the destination: “Lucia runs into the country house,” *Lucia läuft ins Landhaus hinein*.
4. **Under the wagon / unter dem Wagen.** *Plaustro* is ablative singular and gives the place where the boy sits.
5. **About the island / über die Insel.** In the supplied situation, the island is the topic of the old man's account. *Insula* is ablative after *de*. The preposition can express “from” in other contexts; its case alone doesn't choose the meaning.
6. **Preposition:** “with Lucia / mit Lucia.” *Lucia* is ablative after *cum*. The sentence means “Marcus dines with Lucia.”
7. **Subordinating conjunction:** “when Lucia dines / wenn Lucia zu Abend isst.” “While Lucia dines / während Lucia zu Abend isst” also fits this scene. *Lucia cenat* has its own subject and finite verb; the *cum* clause tells us when Marcus reads.
8. **Nam coordinates; quia subordinates.** Both give the reason for Marcus's silence. German **denn** and **weil** make the structural difference useful: *Marcus schweigt, denn der Junge schläft*; *Marcus schweigt, weil der Junge schläft*.
9. **Purpose:** “so that the children can dine / damit die Kinder zu Abend essen können.” *Cenent* is present subjunctive. The mother prepares the food with that aim.
10. **Result:** “so that his friends hear him / sodass seine Freunde ihn hören.” *Audiant* is present subjunctive; *tam clare* means “so loudly.” The sentence presents their hearing as the result of his loud shouting.

Use each mistake to choose the next prompt. If you knew a destination required the accusative but wrote *villa*, practise the **noun form**. If you read *in villa* as “into the house,” return to the **location/destination meaning**. If you translated *quia* as “because” but called it coordinating, practise **clause structure**. Each error points to a different piece of the sentence.

## Return to the difficult distinctions across sessions

Start with a few expressions whose vocabulary you understand. Answer before revealing the back, then check both the direct answer and the explanation. After a mistake, make the correction and try other material before returning to that target. Getting it right immediately after seeing the answer doesn't yet show that you can recall it in a later session.

Return in a later session, including some questions you answered correctly. Regular recall and spaced review can help keep these distinctions available over time. [Roediger and Karpicke's retrieval experiment](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/) found a delayed-retention benefit from testing prose material; [Cepeda and colleagues' spacing study](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x/) found that useful review gaps depended on the later retention interval. Neither study tested this Latin practice set or establishes one universal review timetable.

When a sentence becomes familiar, try a new sentence with the same construction. Keep reading connected Latin too: recalling a case rule doesn't by itself teach you to follow a longer argument or translate an unseen passage. For help writing your own narrow prompts, the [guide to making better flashcards](/blog/how-to-make-better-flashcards/) explains how to keep a front clear and put the direct answer first.
