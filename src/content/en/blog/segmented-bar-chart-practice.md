At a fictional repair café, 32 evening visitors brought clothing, compared with 24 daytime visitors. The evening shift had twice as many visitors overall. Clothing made up 40% of evening visits and 60% of daytime visits. A 100% segmented bar chart shows that difference alongside the share of visits for every other item category.

## Decide what counts as 100%

The café recorded each visitor’s shift and the main item they brought. Those are the two categorical variables. Each visitor counts once in the two-way table.

| Shift | Clothing | Small appliance | Other | Total visitors |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Day | 24 | 12 | 4 | 40 |
| Evening | 32 | 16 | 32 | 80 |

Start with *among whom?* To find the share of **daytime visitors** who brought clothing, divide 24 by 40. To find the share of **evening visitors** who brought clothing, divide 32 by 80. If you want the share of **visitors with clothing** who came during the day, divide the same 24 by 56, the clothing column total. The group named after “among” supplies the denominator.

For the chart, the question is how the mix of items varies by shift. Divide each cell by its shift’s row total. Each share is a **conditional relative frequency**. All three shares together give the **conditional distribution** of item type for that shift. [Penn State’s lesson on categorical variables](https://online.stat.psu.edu/stat100/Lesson06)

| Shift | Clothing | Small appliance | Other | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Day | 60% | 30% | 10% | 100% |
| Evening | 40% | 20% | 40% | 100% |

Each row adds to 100% because every visitor in that shift belongs to exactly one item category. Dividing 24 by all 120 visitors would answer a different question. It gives the share of all visitors who came during the day **and** brought clothing. [Conditional probability practice with two-way tables](/blog/conditional-probability-two-way-tables/)

## Draw and read the segmented bars

In a 100% segmented bar chart, also called a **100% stacked bar chart**, each group gets a bar from 0% to 100%. Start at 0% and mark off each category’s share in turn. Keep the order and colors or patterns consistent, label the categories, and include the number of visitors in each shift.

- **Day.** Clothing runs from 0% to 60%, Small appliance from 60% to 90%, and Other from 90% to 100%.
- **Evening.** Clothing runs from 0% to 40%, Small appliance from 40% to 60%, and Other from 60% to 100%.

![Two stacked bar charts of repair café visits, with visitor counts above and within-shift percentages below. Each chart shows Day and Evening bars split into Clothing, Small appliance, and Other.](https://czaixyl0esghkd4s.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/marketing/8ae7d94ce0a78996480b7050a687e66de2d22af424138c75a92285b13a89271e.webp)

To read a segment, subtract its start from its end. In the Day bar, Small appliance runs from 60% to 90%, so the segment spans 30 percentage points and represents 30% of daytime visitors. The matching segment runs from 40% to 60% in the Evening bar and represents 20% of evening visitors.

## Compare the item mix across shifts

In the top chart, the Evening bar is twice as long as the Day bar, and its Clothing segment is longer because 32 is greater than 24. In the bottom chart, each shift fills a 100% bar. The Clothing segment is longer for Day because 60% is greater than 40%. Here’s how you could describe all three segments in a written answer.

> At the repair café, the share of visits for clothing was 20 percentage points higher during the day (60% versus 40% in the evening). Small appliances also accounted for a larger share during the day (30% versus 20%). The Other category accounted for a larger share in the evening (40% versus 10%).

The chart shows an association between shift and item type in these 120 visits. A claim about what caused the difference or whether it holds at other cafés would need more evidence.

## Practice with two more count tables

For each fictional data set below, find the shift totals, calculate the percentages, and draw two horizontal bars on a 0%–100% scale. Keep the categories in table order. Use the bars to write a comparison that names the groups and item types.

| Exercise | Shift | Clothing | Small appliance | Other |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | Day | 10 | 8 | 2 |
| 1 | Evening | 20 | 4 | 16 |
| 2 | Day | 8 | 6 | 6 |
| 2 | Evening | 20 | 15 | 15 |

1. In Exercise 1, which category takes up the same share in both bars? Where does each Small appliance segment end? Describe how the full item mix differs by shift.
2. In Exercise 2, where are the segment boundaries in each bar? What do the bars tell you about the item mix?

### Answers

1. The totals are 20 for Day and 40 for Evening. The shares are **50%, 40%, 10%** for Day and **50%, 10%, 40%** for Evening. The Clothing segments both end at 50%. Small appliance ends at 90% for Day and 60% for Evening, giving Day the larger share of small appliances. The space left for Other is wider in the Evening bar, so the full distributions differ even though the clothing shares match.
2. The totals are 20 for Day and 50 for Evening. Both shifts are **40%, 30%, 30%**. The boundaries line up at 40% and 70%, so the observed item mix is the same in both shifts. The count table still shows 30 more visitors for Evening.

## Try one question from memory

A segment starts at 55% and ends at 80%. What share does it represent, and how many people is that in a group of 40? Say your answer before checking. The segment spans 25 percentage points, so it represents 25% of the group, or 10 people.
