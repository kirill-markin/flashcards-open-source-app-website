## Why the spelling changes

A **tritone** spans six semitones. In these examples you’ll see it written as a diminished fifth or an augmented fourth.

A dominant seventh chord is a major triad with a minor seventh above its root. In these voicings, the left hand plays the root and the right hand plays the third and seventh, called the **guide tones**. Leaving out the fifth makes those two lines easier to follow.

Spell G7 as G–B–D–F. B is the third and rises to C. F is the seventh and falls to E. The starting notes are six semitones apart. Count the letter names from B to F, including both ends (B, C, D, E, F), and you get a fifth. A fifth of six semitones is a **diminished fifth (d5)**.

Spell D♭7 as D♭–F–A♭–C♭. The two keys swap chord roles. F is now the third and rises to G♭. The lower note, spelled C♭, is now the seventh and falls to B♭. The keys are still six semitones apart, and C to F spans four letter names (C, D, E, F). A fourth of six semitones is an **augmented fourth (A4)**. [Open Music Theory’s guide to counting intervals](https://open-musictheory.github.io/docs/fundamentals/intervals/#diatonic-intervals)

The right-hand pair by itself sounds identical in both spellings on an equal-tempered piano. The rest of the chord gives those two pitches their roles.

## Follow the third and seventh

These examples use the standard stepwise resolution from V7, the dominant seventh chord built on scale degree 5, to the tonic. The **chord third** is the leading tone (scale degree 7) and rises a semitone to scale degree 1. The **chord seventh** is scale degree 4 and falls a step to scale degree 3. [University of Puget Sound on voice leading from V7 to the tonic](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/V7toIVoiceLeading.html)

Try the G7 example with F an octave lower. Play G2 → C3 with your left hand and F3–B3 → E3–C4 with your right. F is still the seventh and falls to E. B is still the third and rises to C. With F below B, the augmented fourth opens out to a minor sixth. [Practice interval inversion](/blog/interval-inversion/)

Now return to B3–F4 over G3 and resolve to **C minor**. Move the bass to C3 and play C4–E♭4 with your right hand. B natural is the raised leading tone in C minor and still rises a semitone to C. F moves down a **whole step** to E♭, the third of C minor. The diminished fifth closes in to a minor third.

## Tritone resolution exercises

For exercises 1–4, spell the dominant seventh chord, then write its third and seventh in the indicated octaves. List the two notes from low to high and name the interval. Resolve each voice by step, then name the resulting interval. Use **M** for major and **m** for minor.

1. **D7 → G major.** Put the third in octave 3 and the seventh in octave 4.
2. **A7 → D major.** Put the third in octave 4 and the seventh in octave 3.
3. **E7 → A minor.** Put both guide tones in octave 4.
4. **B♭7 → E♭ major.** Put both guide tones in octave 4.
5. A student plays the correct keys over D♭3 and writes **B3–F4** for the guide tones of D♭7. Fix the spelling, name the tritone, and show where each voice lands in G♭ major.

## Answer key

1. **D7 is D–F♯–A–C.** F♯3–C4 (d5) resolves to G3–B3 (M3). The third F♯3 rises to G3 and the seventh C4 falls to B3.
2. **A7 is A–C♯–E–G.** G3–C♯4 (A4) resolves to F♯3–D4 (m6). The third C♯4 rises to D4 and the seventh G3 falls to F♯3.
3. **E7 is E–G♯–B–D.** D4–G♯4 (A4) resolves to C4–A4 (M6). The third G♯4 rises to A4 and the seventh D4 falls a whole step to C4. The tonic chord is minor, and C4 to A4 spans nine semitones, a major sixth.
4. **B♭7 is B♭–D–F–A♭.** D4–A♭4 (d5) resolves to E♭4–G4 (M3). The third D4 rises to E♭4 and the seventh A♭4 falls to G4.
5. Write **C♭4–F4 (A4)**. F4 is the third and rises to G♭4. C♭4 is the seventh and falls to B♭3. The result is B♭3–G♭4 (m6).
