Maya plans to return a borrowed printing roller before leaving the workshop. She remembers exactly where it belongs, then walks out without returning it. Knowing what to do and remembering to do it at the right moment are different demands on memory.

**Prospective memory involves remembering to carry out an intention later. Retrospective memory involves retrieving previously learned information or past experiences.** One activity can involve both: remembering that an action is due, then recalling what the action should be.

The scenarios below are invented teaching examples for introductory and AP Psychology learners. They aren't observations of real people or a validated memory test.

## Look for an intention that already existed

The [APA Dictionary defines prospective memory](https://dictionary.apa.org/prospective-memory) around carrying out an intended action after a delay. That earlier intention matters. Simply doing something later in the day doesn't establish prospective remembering.

For a scenario question, identify three things:

1. **Intention:** What did the person previously decide to do?
2. **Cue:** What time or event should bring that intention back?
3. **Content:** What information must the person recall to carry it out?

For retrospective memory, the question can instead ask for information now: a definition, a room number, or what happened during a conversation. The information was acquired earlier, even if it describes a future event.

Remembering *tomorrow's workshop starts at four* is retrospective retrieval when someone asks for the start time now. Remembering *to leave for the workshop at three* after forming that plan earlier is a prospective task. The word “tomorrow” doesn't settle the classification.

Prospective memory appears in Topic 2.3 of the current [AP Psychology Course and Exam Description](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-psychology-course-and-exam-description.pdf). For application questions, explaining what the person must remember is more useful than matching “future” to a label.

## Separate two demands in the same errand

At the start of a printmaking session, Maya decides: “When the cleanup bell rings, I'll return the borrowed roller to the green storage box.” She then works on a print.

| Question about the same plan | Memory demand |
| --- | --- |
| At the bell, does Maya remember that she has an action to carry out? | Prospective component |
| Does she recall which object to return and where it belongs? | Retrospective component |

This distinction follows the two-component account described in [Cohen and colleagues' research introduction](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/07/CohenDixonLindsayMasson2003.pdf): remembering at the appropriate moment that something must be done, and recalling what must be done.

Now change the outcome while keeping Maya's original plan:

- **The bell rings, and Maya remembers to return the roller to the green box.** The task uses both components successfully.
- **The bell reminds her that something needs returning, but she can't recall which object or destination.** The prospective component has brought the intention to mind; the missing action details concern its retrospective component.
- **At the bell, the intention doesn't come to mind. Later, when asked, she correctly recalls the entire plan.** This account describes a failure to remember to act at the cue, despite later recall of the content. Later recall doesn't prove that the content was accessible at every earlier moment.

These are different versions of a hypothetical scenario, not experimental results. They show why “prospective or retrospective?” can be too broad unless the question specifies the task or component.

## Time cues and event cues

**Time-based prospective memory** concerns acting at a particular time or after an interval. **Event-based prospective memory** concerns acting when a particular event occurs. The [APA TOPSS memory lesson, pages 29–30](https://www.apa.org/ed/precollege/topss/lessons/memory.pdf), explains these forms and describes both monitoring and spontaneous retrieval of intentions.

Change Maya's plan to “Return the roller at 16:00,” and the target is a time. Keep “Return it when the cleanup bell rings,” and the target is an event. Even if the bell normally rings at 16:00, classify the cue specified in her intention.

An intention can come back while someone checks for the relevant cue or without deliberate monitoring at that moment. A scenario doesn't need to describe constant rehearsal to count as prospective memory. It does need a previously formed intention that must be remembered later.

## A missed action doesn't explain itself

“Maya didn't return the roller” tells you the outcome. It doesn't establish whether she forgot to act, forgot what to return, chose to keep working, or couldn't reach the box.

If the account only reports noncompletion, say the cause is unresolved. A useful answer names the missing evidence: Did the intention come to mind at the cue? Could she recall its content? Was she able and willing to act?

The same care helps with [negative reinforcement vs punishment](/blog/negative-reinforcement-vs-punishment/): classify the relationship the scenario actually describes. A familiar word or visible outcome may supply only part of the answer.

## Try ten mixed memory questions

Before reading the answers, identify the earlier intention, cue, and remembered content where they exist. Classify the specific demand asked about; use “both” or “insufficient evidence” when appropriate.

1. Before a film club meets, Eli decides to collect the attendance card when the projector is switched off. After the screening, switching off the projector brings that intention to mind. What kind of prospective cue is involved?
2. At breakfast, Sara plans to photograph a model at 15:30. She later notices that time on a clock and remembers to take the photograph. Is this time-based or event-based prospective memory?
3. Someone asks Omar where next week's rehearsal will take place. He recalls the room number he learned yesterday. Is this prospective or retrospective retrieval?
4. Earlier, Lina planned to place a sample in compartment 6 when the supervisor arrived. The arrival reminds her to put the sample away, but she can't recall the compartment number. Which component is missing?
5. Theo planned to switch off a display when the gallery doors closed. The doors close, but the intention doesn't come to mind. Later, asked what his plan was, he gives it correctly. Which failure is explicitly described?
6. At noon, Mei decides to give a visitor a folded map when the visitor returns. That afternoon, the return brings the plan to mind, and Mei recalls and hands over the correct map. Does the task involve one memory component or both?
7. Seeing an empty noticeboard, Pavel decides for the first time to put up a poster. He recalls where he stored it yesterday and puts it up straight away. Has the account demonstrated prospective remembering?
8. Ruth intended to bring a stencil when the craft session began. The session begins without the stencil. Nothing else is reported. Can you identify which memory component failed?
9. On a flashcard, Inez reads “What does prospective memory mean?” She recalls the definition learned last week. What kind of retrieval is she performing at that moment?
10. Ben plans to stretch when a recording ends. Ana plans to stretch after twenty minutes have elapsed. Each later remembers the plan and recalls the intended stretch. How do their cues differ, and do both tasks contain retrospective content?

## Check the explanation, not just the label

1. **Event-based prospective memory.** Switching off the projector is the target event. Eli formed the collection intention beforehand, then remembered it at that event. A screening taking time doesn't make the specified cue time-based.
2. **Time-based prospective memory.** Sara's earlier intention specifies 15:30. Looking at a clock supplies information about whether that target time has arrived; the plan isn't keyed to a separate event such as someone entering.
3. **Retrospective retrieval.** Omar retrieves previously learned information in response to a question now. The rehearsal's future date doesn't turn the room-number question into remembering to execute an intention later.
4. **The retrospective component of the prospective task.** Lina remembers that the arrival calls for action, but lacks a stored detail needed to complete it. Calling the whole situation “retrospective only” would overlook the successful prospective component.
5. **Failure of the prospective component at the cue.** Theo doesn't remember to act when the doors close. His later correct account shows later retrieval of the plan; it doesn't erase the missed cue or establish his memory state throughout the interval.
6. **Both components.** The visitor's return cues the delayed intention, and Mei recalls the intended action and object. Calling this an event-based prospective task is compatible with recognizing its retrospective content.
7. **No delayed intention has been demonstrated.** Pavel forms the poster intention on the spot and acts immediately. Recalling the poster's stored location is retrospective retrieval. An action occurring now isn't automatically the execution of an earlier plan.
8. **Insufficient evidence.** Ruth's prospective task wasn't completed, but the account doesn't isolate why. She could have missed the cue, forgotten the stencil detail, or failed to bring it for a reason unrelated to memory.
9. **Retrospective retrieval.** The concept being recalled is prospective memory; recalling its definition is a different task. The question directly asks Inez for learned information rather than requiring her to remember to initiate a delayed action.
10. **Ben has an event cue; Ana has a time interval. Both tasks also involve retrospective content.** The end of the recording cues Ben's plan; twenty elapsed minutes define Ana's target. Each must recall what action was intended.

## Put the reasoning on the back of the card

For a missed question, write a short scenario on a flashcard and ask about one specific demand. On the back, explain the intention, cue, and content. Include a case where “both” is correct and another where the evidence is insufficient. The [guide to making better flashcards](/blog/how-to-make-better-flashcards/) can help you keep prompts focused.

For broader course practice, Nibomo's English [AP Psychology deck](/catalog/packages/ap-psychology-flashcards/) contains 705 cards, including a prospective-memory definition. It's a course resource rather than a copy of these ten questions. Recalling concepts regularly, with reviews spread over time, helps you retain that learned information in long-term memory. That doesn't guarantee remembering errands or completing an intended action outside a study session.

Before choosing a label in a new scenario, write what must be remembered. “Do this when the cue arrives” and “recall this information now” describe different demands, and a single planned activity can contain both.
