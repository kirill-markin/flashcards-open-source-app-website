D major's relative minor is B minor. Its parallel minor is D minor. You can calculate either one correctly and still answer the wrong question if you miss that single word.

**Relative minor keeps the key signature. Parallel minor keeps the tonic.** Decide what stays fixed before counting notes. Then check the partner's spelling and signature.

The examples below use English music-theory terminology and standard Western major and minor keys. Start with D major, then try a flat key where keeping the tonic produces a less familiar answer.

![A florist lifts a rust-orange dahlia into focus within a bouquet of cream and pale flowers](/blog/relative-vs-parallel-minor.png)

## What stays the same?

The **tonic** is a key's home note. The **key signature** sets the default sharps or flats for its written notes; it can also contain none, as in C major and A minor.

| Relationship | What stays fixed | What changes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relative major and minor | Key signature | Tonic | D major and B minor: both have two sharps |
| Parallel major and minor | Tonic | Key signature | D major and D minor: both have D as tonic |

These are the relationships defined in [Belmont University's key-relationships guide](https://www.belmont.edu/cmpa/_files/studyguide-key-relatinships.pdf). The major and natural minor scales of relative keys use the same notes, ordered from different tonics. Parallel major and natural minor scales begin on the same note but differ at three scale degrees.

Say “same signature” or “same tonic” before working out the answer. A question asking only for “the minor of D major” needs a more precise relationship.

## Find the relative minor inside the major scale

Count to **degree 6 of the major scale**, with the tonic as degree 1. Keep every sharp or flat attached to that note.

D major is:

**D–E–F♯–G–A–B–C♯–D**

The sixth degree is B, so its relative minor is **B minor**. Both key signatures contain F♯ and C♯. Starting that collection on B gives B natural minor:

**B–C♯–D–E–F♯–G–A–B**

Now try A♭ major:

**A♭–B♭–C–D♭–E♭–F–G–A♭**

Degree 6 is F. The relative minor is **F minor**, and both keys have four flats: B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭. F natural minor is:

**F–G–A♭–B♭–C–D♭–E♭–F**

The starting major key has a flat in its name; the relative minor doesn't. Read the sixth degree from the scale instead of copying the tonic's accidental.

For the reverse direction, the **third degree of a natural minor scale** names its relative major. In the F minor scale above, degree 3 is A♭, taking you back to A♭ major.

A shorter route is a minor third down from the major tonic: three semitones with the correct letter spelling. For D, count the letters downward D–C–B, then check the distance to B. If that shortcut makes you hesitate over accidentals, use the major scale's sixth degree.

## Parallel minor keeps the whole tonic name

D major's parallel minor is **D minor**. A♭ major's parallel minor is **A♭ minor**. Keep the sharp or flat in the tonic's name too.

To find the notes and signature, lower major-scale degrees **3, 6, and 7 by one semitone each**, keeping their letters. The result is the parallel natural minor scale. Minor key signatures follow the natural minor form, as explained in [Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/MinorKeySignatures.html).

Here is the construction for D:

| Degree | D major | D natural minor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | D | D |
| 2 | E | E |
| 3 | F♯ | F |
| 4 | G | G |
| 5 | A | A |
| 6 | B | B♭ |
| 7 | C♯ | C |

D natural minor is **D–E–F–G–A–B♭–C–D**. Only B is flat, so D minor's signature has one flat, B♭.

Watch what “lower” does here. F♯ becomes F natural; C♯ becomes C natural. Adding flat signs to those letters would produce F♭ and C♭, each two semitones below the original sharp. Lower the pitch by one semitone, then write the accidental that gives you that pitch.

For A♭ major, the third, sixth, and seventh degrees are C, F, and G. Lower them to C♭, F♭, and G♭:

**A♭–B♭–C♭–D♭–E♭–F♭–G♭–A♭**

A♭ minor has seven flats. All seven letters are flat, even C and F. On an equal-tempered piano, C♭ sounds like B and F♭ sounds like E, but those substitutions would lose the scale's one-letter-per-degree spelling.

G♯ minor is an enharmonic alternative to A♭ minor: its scale sounds the same on that piano. For this written exercise, keep A♭ minor because the requested tonic is A♭.

| Starting key | Relative minor: same signature | Parallel minor: same tonic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D major, two sharps | B minor, two sharps | D minor, one flat |
| A♭ major, four flats | F minor, four flats | A♭ minor, seven flats |

## The signature doesn't tell you which note is home

Two sharps give you D major and B minor as candidates in a major/minor exercise. Choosing between them takes musical context: phrase endings, supporting chords, and the note that sounds settled. The first or last melody note is a clue, not enough evidence by itself.

Try this on a keyboard, playing each three-note chord together:

- Play **D–F♯–A**, then D major's scale, then the chord again.
- Play **B–D–F♯**, then B natural minor's scale, then its chord again.
- Return to **D–F♯–A**, then change it to **D–F–A**. Play D natural minor and finish on D–F–A.

The first two examples share a signature and move the tonic from D to B. The last comparison keeps D as tonic while changing major to minor. Listen for both differences. These short examples illustrate the relationships; real passages can take more context to establish a key.

A minor key can also use notes outside its natural minor scale. Harmonic minor raises degree 7; the conventional ascending melodic minor scale raises degrees 6 and 7, each by a semitone from natural minor. Those changes appear as accidentals in the music without changing the key signature. [Open Music Theory explains the three minor-scale forms](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/minor-scales/).

B harmonic minor, for example, has A♯, while B minor's key signature still contains F♯ and C♯. Its relative major remains D major. “Harmonic” describes a minor-scale form; “relative” describes a relationship between keys.

## Mixed practice: build, classify, and repair

Cover the answers. For each key you construct, give its full name and key signature. Include a reason so you can distinguish a wrong relationship from a missed accidental.

1. Find the relative minor of F major.
2. Find the parallel minor of A major.
3. Find the relative major of C minor.
4. Starting from E♭ major, give both its relative minor and its parallel minor.
5. Are B♭ major and G minor relative, parallel, or neither?
6. A student writes, “B major's relative minor is G minor: G is the sixth letter.” Repair the answer.
7. Another student writes D–E–F♭–G–A–B♭–C♭–D for D natural minor. Repair the two wrong notes.
8. A passage has one flat in its signature. Is that enough evidence to call it F major?
9. Find the parallel major of F♯ minor. Does it keep the minor key's signature?
10. C harmonic minor contains B natural. Does that make C major its relative major?

### Answers and reasons

1. **D minor; one flat, B♭.** D is F major's sixth degree. The signature stays the same.
2. **A minor; no sharps or flats.** Keep A as tonic. Lower A major's C♯, F♯, and G♯ to C, F, and G.
3. **E♭ major; three flats, B♭, E♭, A♭.** E♭ is degree 3 of C natural minor. Keep its flat.
4. **Relative: C minor, three flats. Parallel: E♭ minor, six flats.** C is degree 6 of E♭ major. C minor keeps B♭, E♭, A♭. For E♭ natural minor, lower G, C, and D to G♭, C♭, and D♭; its signature is B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭.
5. **Relative.** Both signatures contain B♭ and E♭. G is degree 6 of B♭ major, and the tonics differ.
6. **G♯ minor; five sharps, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯.** B major is B–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G♯–A♯–B. Its sixth degree is G♯, including the sharp.
7. **F♭ becomes F; C♭ becomes C.** Lowering D major's F♯ and C♯ by one semitone cancels each sharp. D natural minor is D–E–F–G–A–B♭–C–D.
8. **No; D minor shares the signature.** Read the harmony and phrase endings, and listen for the tonic. The signature alone leaves both major/minor candidates open.
9. **F♯ major; six sharps, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯.** Parallel keeps F♯ as tonic. F♯ minor has three sharps, so the signature changes. E♯ is the seventh degree of F♯ major; writing F natural would repeat the tonic's letter.
10. **No; the relative major remains E♭ major, with three flats.** B natural is C harmonic minor's raised seventh. C major is C minor's parallel major.

## Make a card for the step you missed

A wrong answer tells you what to practice. Confusing the two relationships calls for a different prompt from forgetting a sharp.

| Missed step | Card prompt | Answer to require |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chose the wrong relationship | A major → parallel minor. What stays fixed? | A stays the tonic; A minor. |
| Dropped an accidental | Relative minor of B major? Show degree 6. | G♯ minor; B–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G♯. |
| Misread “lower” | Lower F♯ by one semitone, keeping the letter. | F natural. |
| Guessed from the signature | One flat: which major/minor pair? | F major and D minor; musical context distinguishes them. |

Make a small mixed set from your mistakes. Answer each card before revealing it, including the reason. After an error, correct it and solve the same kind of question with a different starting key. In your next study session, shuffle the prompts so their order doesn't give away the rule. The [music theory flashcards guide](/blog/music-theory-flashcards/) extends this approach to notation, intervals, and chords.

Then take one pair to your instrument. Use D major and B minor to practice changing the tonic with a shared signature, or D major and D minor to keep the tonic while changing the scale. A written answer checks the relationship and spelling; playing and listening give you another way to recognize what changed.
