On Monday, the library lent Noor two recorders. A workshop update might start with Noor. An equipment log might start with the recorders. The passive voice with two objects lets you report the same loan either way. To choose a subject, look at what the reader is already following.

## Both objects can become the subject

In “The library lent Noor two recorders on Monday,” *Noor* is the **indirect object**, the person receiving the recorders. *Two recorders* is the **direct object**, what the library lent. The library is the subject of the active sentence and the lender. *Lend*, *give*, *send* and *offer* can all take two objects in this order. See [Cambridge’s guide to verb patterns](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/verb-patterns-with-and-without-).

| Start with | Sentence |
| --- | --- |
| The lender, in the active voice | **The library** lent Noor two recorders on Monday. |
| The recipient, in the passive voice | **Noor** was lent two recorders by the library on Monday. |
| The recorders, in the passive voice | **Two recorders** were lent to Noor by the library on Monday. |

Both passives describe a loan that happened on Monday. Use *was lent* with singular *Noor* and *were lent* with plural *two recorders*. When the recorders are the subject, *to Noor* tells you who received them. In either version, *by the library* tells you who lent them. For another pair of examples, see [Cambridge’s guide to passive forms](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/passive-forms).

### Put the same loan in two messages

In a library staff update, the coordinator is writing about Noor.

> Noor is recording interviews for the library workshop this week. **She was lent two recorders on Monday.**

*She* picks up *Noor*. The second sentence tells us what equipment she got.

In the library’s equipment log, the entry is about the recorders.

> The two recorders from shelf B are due back on Friday. **They were lent to Noor on Monday.**

*They* picks up *the two recorders*. The second sentence tells us who borrowed them.

Either subject choice gives a grammatical passive. In these examples, starting with the person or equipment already mentioned makes the second sentence easier to follow. The writers can leave out *by the library* because the source of the loan is clear. See [Cambridge’s guide to passives with and without an agent](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/passives).

## Write both versions before you choose

1. Find the doer, the recipient and the thing in the active sentence. An offer has a recipient even if they haven’t accepted it yet.
2. With the two-object uses of *lend*, *give*, *send* and *offer* here, start one passive with the recipient. Start the other with the thing and put the recipient after *to*.
3. Read the previous sentence. Which subject lets you continue what it was saying? If you’re answering a question, see who or what the question asks about.
4. Check that the verb agrees with the new subject and keeps the original tense. Keep the time expression. If you use *to* or *by*, check that *to* introduces the recipient and *by* introduces the doer. For more work on the verb phrase, try [passive voice tense practice](/blog/passive-voice-tense-practice/).

### With *explain*, start with what was explained

In “The instructor explained the rules to me,” *the rules* is the direct object. The listener appears in the phrase *to me*. For this pattern, make the rules the subject and keep the listener after *to*. The passive is “The rules were explained to me by the instructor.” See [Cambridge’s grammar guide to *explain*](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/explain).

## Six exercises with answers

Keep the doer in a *by* phrase for these exercises so you can check everyone’s role. Use a pronoun when the previous sentence makes its meaning clear.

1. The guide gave us two maps before the tour. Write both passives.
2. The organizers sent Rosa the room number yesterday. Write both passives, then choose the one that fits better after “Rosa is getting ready for the meeting.”
3. Use the same two passives from exercise 2. Which one fits better after “The room number is already on the schedule”? Write it as the next sentence.
4. Sam gave Lea the keys this morning. A note says “The keys were given to Sam by Lea this morning.” Correct the note and keep *the keys* as the subject.
5. The instructor explained the safety rules to us before class. Repair this draft passive. “We were explained the safety rules by the instructor before class.”
6. The clinic will offer Dan two appointment times tomorrow. Write both passives. Which one fits after “Dan is waiting to hear from the clinic”?

### Answer key

1. **We were given two maps by the guide before the tour.** **Two maps were given to us by the guide before the tour.** Use *we* as the subject and *us* after *to*. Both subjects are plural, so both use *were given*.
2. **Rosa was sent the room number by the organizers yesterday.** **The room number was sent to Rosa by the organizers yesterday.** Both keep *yesterday* and use *was sent* with a singular subject. The first fits better here because the previous sentence is about Rosa. You can write “She was sent the room number by the organizers yesterday” in that context.
3. **It was sent to Rosa by the organizers yesterday.** *It* refers to the room number and keeps the focus on that piece of information. Either form from exercise 2 is grammatical.
4. **The keys were given to Lea by Sam this morning.** *To Lea* names the recipient and *by Sam* names the giver. The draft is grammatical. It says Lea gave the keys to Sam.
5. **The safety rules were explained to us by the instructor before class.** With *explain*, the rules become the subject and the listeners stay in the phrase *to us*. The plural subject takes *were explained*.
6. **Dan will be offered two appointment times by the clinic tomorrow.** **Two appointment times will be offered to Dan by the clinic tomorrow.** Both keep *will* and use *be offered*. The first fits better because the previous sentence is about Dan. You could start that version with *he* once Dan has been named.
