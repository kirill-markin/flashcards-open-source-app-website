E augmented is E–G♯–B♯. That last note can look strange if you're picturing a piano: B♯ lands on the C key. But write E–G♯–C on a chord-spelling exercise and you've lost the fifth. The letter C is a sixth above E.

**Triad chord spelling needs two checks: the right letters and the right distances.** Writing every other letter handles the first. Counting half steps handles the second. Put those together and you can build major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads without memorizing a page of chord shapes.

The method below uses common Western note names and ascending intervals. You'll build chords in root–third–fifth order, then try a short worksheet that asks you to explain errors as well as produce answers.

## Start with three letters

A triad can be arranged as two consecutive thirds. In that arrangement, its notes are the root, third, and fifth. Those names describe each note's relationship to the root. [Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom introduces triads this way](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/TriadsIntroduction.html).

For a chord on E, count letter names upward, including E as one: **E (1), F (2), G (3), A (4), B (5)**. Continue from G back to A whenever you reach the end of the musical alphabet.

Keep positions 1, 3, and 5: **E–G–B**. Every E triad in this lesson uses those letters. Its quality determines the accidentals.

A flat or sharp on the root doesn't change the letter pattern. D♭ starts with D–F–A; F♯ starts with F–A–C. Keep the root's given accidental while working out the other two notes.

This is a useful place to catch yourself. If your answer for a D♭ chord contains E instead of some kind of F, fix the letters before counting anything else.

## The four patterns to check

Measure both intervals **from the root**. A semitone, also called a half step, is the distance to the next key on a piano, including black keys. Count the moves between keys: the starting pitch is zero steps away from itself.

In the table, M means major, m means minor, P means perfect, d means diminished, and A means augmented. The number gives the interval's letter size: M3 is a major third; P5 is a perfect fifth.

| Quality | Third | Fifth | Half steps from root |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major | M3 | P5 | 0, 4, 7 |
| Minor | m3 | P5 | 0, 3, 7 |
| Diminished | m3 | d5 | 0, 3, 6 |
| Augmented | M3 | A5 | 0, 4, 8 |

You may also learn stacked-third patterns: major is 4 then 3 semitones; minor is 3 then 4; diminished is 3 then 3; augmented is 4 then 4. [The university text's triad-construction summary](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/SummaryOfTriadConstruction.html) shows those pairs. They describe the same chords: add the two distances to get the fifth's distance from the root. In the table above, the final 7 for a major triad starts at the root, not at its third.

To spell a chord:

1. Write the root and its two alternating letter names.
2. Choose the quality's semitone pattern.
3. Add accidentals to the third and fifth until both distances match.
4. Read the letters again, then check the distances independently.

For a keyboard-free check, the natural-note gaps are C–D = 2, D–E = 2, E–F = 1, F–G = 2, G–A = 2, A–B = 2, and B–C = 1 semitone. A sharp raises a natural note by one semitone; a flat lowers it by one. Remember to account for the root's accidental too.

## Work through an unfamiliar root

Try **D♭ minor**. Its letter pattern is D–F–A, and minor needs 3 and 7 semitones above the root.

D to F is three semitones: D–E contributes two, and E–F contributes one. Starting a semitone lower on D♭ makes the distance to F **four**, so F is too high for a minor third. Lower it to **F♭**.

For the fifth, D to A is seven semitones. D♭ to A is therefore eight; lower A to **A♭** to make seven. The result is **D♭–F♭–A♭**. Read it back in two passes: D–F–A gives the right letters, and the accidentals give 3 and 7 semitones above D♭.

F♭ is intentional. On an equal-tempered piano, F♭ and E have the same pitch, but the third above D must have an F letter name. Enharmonic names can refer to the same piano key while occupying different positions on the staff. [The university text's enharmonic-notes lesson](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/EnharmonicNotes.html) includes these white-key spellings too.

Now compare all four qualities on A. Keeping the same root makes the changes easy to see:

| Chord | Spelling | Check from A |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A major | A–C♯–E | 4, 7 |
| A minor | A–C–E | 3, 7 |
| A diminished | A–C–E♭ | 3, 6 |
| A augmented | A–C♯–E♯ | 4, 8 |

Notice what stays fixed: the letters A–C–E. Starting from the major triad, lower the third one semitone for minor; lower both the third and fifth for diminished; raise the fifth for augmented. “Lower the fifth” doesn't automatically mean “add a flat.” Lowering C♯ by one semitone produces C natural.

Chord symbols may shorten these names to A, Am, A° and A+. A bare root symbol means major in this convention; ° marks diminished and + marks augmented. [The lead-sheet symbols lesson](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/LeadSheetSymbols.html) shows how root and quality combine in these labels.

## Find the error before rewriting the chord

Suppose the prompt is **F♯ major** and the answer is F♯–B♭–C♯. On an equal-tempered piano, these pitches match F♯ major. The spelling still fails: F–B–C isn't a stack of letter thirds. The middle letter must be A, so use **F♯–A♯–C♯**.

Compare that with **F♯–A–C♯**. Its letters pass, but its third is only three semitones above F♯. That's an accidental error for this prompt, and the written chord is F♯ minor.

Give yourself a specific correction:

- **Wrong letter:** rewrite the 1–3–5 letter pattern, then choose the accidental.
- **Right letter, wrong distance:** keep the letter and change its accidental.
- **Both distances seem wrong:** check that you counted from the actual root, including its sharp or flat, with zero at the starting pitch.

This also explains why a piano alone can't grade spelling. It can confirm pitches while hiding an enharmonic letter error.

### When the answer needs a double accidental

This is optional extension work. **B augmented** starts with B–D–F and needs distances of 4 and 8 semitones. D♯ gives the third. F♯ gives only seven semitones above B, so the fifth must rise again: **F𝄪**, F double-sharp. The answer is **B–D♯–F𝄪**.

F𝄪 is two semitones above F natural and shares a piano key with G in equal temperament. Substituting G would replace the required fifth letter with a sixth letter. The spelling rule keeps working even when the accidentals become less familiar.

A double-flat works the other way: 𝄫 lowers a natural note by two semitones. A𝄫 is one semitone below A♭ and shares a piano key with G. You'll need that distinction for the optional exercise below.

## Triad practice with answers

Use paper and cover the answer section. For each construction, write the letter pattern, the finished chord, and the two distances from the root. For each repair, name the error before correcting it. No key signature is implied; write all needed accidentals.

| --- | --- |
| 1 | Spell E♭ major. |
| 2 | Spell B♭ minor. |
| 3 | Spell C♯ diminished. |
| 4 | Spell G augmented. |
| 5 | Spell F♯ minor. |
| 6 | Spell A♭ augmented. |
| 7 | Name the chord D–F–A♭, given in root–third–fifth order. |
| 8 | Name the chord B–D♯–F♯, given in root–third–fifth order. |
| 9 | Repair D major written as D–G♭–A. |
| 10 | Repair C diminished written as C–E♭–G. |
| 11 | Repair E augmented written as E–G♯–C. |
| 12 | Optional: spell D♭ diminished, including any double accidental. |

### Check your reasoning

| # | Answer | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | E♭–G–B♭ | E–G–B letters; 4 and 7 semitones above E♭. |
| 2 | B♭–D♭–F | B–D–F letters; 3 and 7 semitones above B♭. |
| 3 | C♯–E–G | C–E–G letters; 3 and 6 above C♯. G needs no flat. |
| 4 | G–B–D♯ | G–B–D letters; 4 and 8 above G. |
| 5 | F♯–A–C♯ | F–A–C letters; 3 and 7 above F♯. |
| 6 | A♭–C–E | A–C–E letters; 4 and 8 above A♭. Raising E♭ produces E natural. |
| 7 | D diminished | D to F is 3; D to A♭ is 6. |
| 8 | B major | B to D♯ is 4; B to F♯ is 7. |
| 9 | D–F♯–A | Wrong letter: D's third needs F. F♯ replaces G♭ at the same pitch. |
| 10 | C–E♭–G♭ | Wrong accidental: lower the fifth from 7 to 6 semitones. |
| 11 | E–G♯–B♯ | Wrong letter: E's fifth needs B. B♯ is 8 semitones above E. |
| 12 | D♭–F♭–A𝄫 | D–F–A letters; 3 and 6 above D♭. Lower A♭ once more to A𝄫. |

If you missed 9 or 11 but got the construction questions right, spend your next practice round explaining why a same-key substitution fails. If you missed 3 or 6, check what happens when the root already has an accidental. Adding a flat for every diminished fifth or a sharp for every augmented fifth won't work.

## Keep the root separate from the bass

The exercises give you root–third–fifth order on purpose. In a score, the lowest sounding note is the **bass**, and it needn't be the root. C♯–E–A, played with C♯ lowest, is A major in first inversion: the third is in the bass. Rearranging those note names into A–C♯–E reveals the triad you already know.

When analyzing a voicing, identify the root and quality, then check which chord member is in the bass. [The university text's chord-analysis lesson](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/AnalyzingChords.html) treats these as separate questions. Don't apply a root-position semitone pattern directly from an arbitrary bass note.

## Turn a recurring miss into a useful card

Once you can construct the answers, practice recalling them. The [triad chord spelling flashcards](/catalog/packages/triad-chord-spelling-flashcards/) contain 90 English cards: four quality rules and 43 root-position chords in both directions, from chord name to notes and from notes to chord name.

The deck includes major and minor triads on C, D♭, D, E♭, E, F, F♯, G, A♭, A, B♭, and B. On those same roots, it includes diminished and augmented triads wherever the spelling needs no double accidentals.

The five exclusions are D♭ diminished, E♭ diminished, A♭ diminished, F♯ augmented, and B augmented. Those require double accidentals, so the extension examples in this article go beyond the deck. It also doesn't cover every enharmonic root, inversions, audio recognition, or staff-reading practice.

For an error the deck doesn't directly diagnose, make one small repair card:

```text
Front: A♭ augmented was written A♭–C–F♭.
       Correct the spelling and explain the change.

Back: A♭–C–E.
      The fifth above A needs the letter E.
      E is 8 semitones above A♭; F♭ uses the wrong letter,
      even though it shares E's pitch on an equal-tempered piano.
```

Keep the explanation on the answer side and say it before revealing the card. The broader [music theory flashcards guide](/blog/music-theory-flashcards/) covers building these targeted cards alongside notation and interval practice.

Then take one chord you missed, write it without help, and play or sing its notes if you can. Written recall, hearing, and performance each need practice. For this exercise, the useful milestone is simple: you can show why every letter and accidental belongs there.
