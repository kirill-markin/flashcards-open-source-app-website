## Which way do you transpose?

**Concert pitch** is the pitch that sounds. **Written pitch** is the note the player reads. For these four instruments in standard treble clef, transpose up to write a part from concert pitch. Transpose down to check what the part will sound like. [Macalester College’s guide to instrument transpositions](https://pressbooks.macalester.digital/multimodalmusicianship/chapter/instrument-transpositions-ranges/)

| Instrument | Concert → written | Written → concert |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B♭ soprano clarinet | Up a major second (2 semitones) | Down a major second |
| B♭ tenor sax | Up a major ninth (14 semitones) | Down a major ninth |
| E♭ alto sax | Up a major sixth (9 semitones) | Down a major sixth |
| Horn in F | Up a perfect fifth (7 semitones) | Down a perfect fifth |

C4 is middle C. The octave number changes at C, so one letter step up from B3 lands on C4. These note names give the pitches directly. B4 means B natural, B♭4 means B-flat, and ♮ calls attention to a natural.

Count letter positions first, including the starting note. Then count semitones to choose any sharp or flat you need. E–F and B–C are one semitone apart. The other gaps between neighboring natural notes are two. A sharp raises a natural by one semitone, and a flat lowers it by one.

## Fix the rehearsal parts

The piano plays **B♭3, C4, D4, E♮4** in B♭ major. The key signature gives you E♭ by default. The natural sign raises that E♭ to E♮, and each part needs to carry that semitone change.

Start with the clarinet's first note. A major second above B♭3 uses the next letter, C. Count one semitone from B♭3 to B3 and one more from B3 to C4. That gives you written **C4**.

### Answer key

| Corrected part | Note 1 | Note 2 | Note 3 | Note 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Piano, concert pitch | B♭3 | C4 | D4 | E♮4 |
| B♭ soprano clarinet, written | C4 | D4 | E4 | F♯4 |
| B♭ tenor sax, written | **C5** | D5 | E5 | F♯5 |
| E♭ alto sax, written | G4 | A4 | B4 | **C♯5** |
| Horn in F, written | F4 | G4 | A4 | **B♮4** |

- **Tenor, note 1.** A major ninth is a major second plus an octave. Lowering C4 by a major second gives B♭3. Lowering it one more octave gives B♭2, an octave below the piano. Moving the tenor's written note up to C5 fixes it. [Practice spelling compound intervals](/blog/compound-intervals-practice/)
- **Alto, note 4.** A major sixth below C5 is E♭4. To get E♮4, count up six letters from E through C. E4 to C5 is eight semitones, so raise the C to C♯5 to make nine.
- **Horn, note 4.** A perfect fifth below B♭4 is E♭4. Count down five letters from B through E. B♮4 down to E♮4 spans seven semitones and gives the piano pitch. If you're using an F major key signature for the horn, write a natural sign to cancel the B♭.
- **Alto, alternate spelling.** Count six letters down from D through F. Nine semitones below D♭5 gives F♭4. In equal temperament, F♭4 and E♮4 share a piano key. Use C♯5 in the part to recover the original E spelling when you transpose back.
