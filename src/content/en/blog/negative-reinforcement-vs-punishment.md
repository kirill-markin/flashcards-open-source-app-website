Taking something away can make a person repeat an action more often. It can also make them repeat it less often. The word *negative* tells you something was removed; it doesn't tell you which way the behavior went.

**Negative reinforcement increases the future likelihood of a behavior by removing or preventing an aversive stimulus: something the person finds unpleasant. Punishment decreases the future likelihood of a behavior.** Punishment can involve either adding something or taking something away. An unpleasant situation alone doesn't decide the answer.

The examples below are original, simplified hypotheticals for introductory psychology. When an example says a consequence caused a change, take that relationship as given. In real life, seeing two events happen in sequence is not enough to establish causation.

![A walker pauses on a coastal path with a small pebble in his palm and a walking shoe in his other hand](/blog/negative-reinforcement-vs-punishment.png)

## Make two separate decisions

Start with the effect on the behavior. Then identify what happened to the stimulus: something the person encounters, such as a sound, an object, or access to an activity.

| Consequence | Behavior becomes more likely | Behavior becomes less likely |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Something is added | Positive reinforcement | Positive punishment |
| Something is removed | Negative reinforcement | Negative punishment |

Here, *positive* means addition and *negative* means removal. They aren't judgments about whether an action is kind, fair, or sensible. These are the two classification axes used in [OpenStax's explanation of operant conditioning](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/6-3-operant-conditioning). Prevention also matters for negative reinforcement; we'll work through that below.

For each problem, fill in this sentence before choosing a label:

> For **[person]**, **[specific action]** becomes **[more or less likely]** because it **[adds, removes, or prevents a specific stimulus]**.

Write “not stated” in any blank the scenario doesn't support. That is more useful than guessing the missing detail. The consequence must depend on the action; merely happening afterward doesn't establish the connection.

## Follow one action from beginning to end

Imagine Ana walking along a beach. Sand gets into her sandals and rubs against her feet. Shaking out the sandals removes the irritating sand. Because of that relief, Ana becomes more likely to shake them out when sand gets in during later walks.

| Part of the account | What to track |
| --- | --- |
| Person | Ana |
| Target action | Shaking sand out of her sandals |
| Consequence of that action | Irritating sand is removed |
| Later effect on the same action | Ana is more likely to shake out her sandals when sand gets in |
| Classification | Negative reinforcement |

Calling this negative punishment would get the removal right but the behavior change wrong. Shaking out the sandals increases.

Walking along the beach is another action in the story, but we aren't told whether it changes. Keep following the action the question asks about, even when several actions appear in the same account.

## Change one detail and reconsider the answer

Comparing similar cases can expose which clue you're actually using.

### The same removal, a different effect

In a hypothetical drawing app, pressing “clear” removes the current sketch from the screen.

- **Version A:** Eli finds the cluttered sketch irritating. Its disappearance causes Eli to press “clear” more often when a sketch becomes cluttered. That removal negatively reinforces clearing the screen.
- **Version B:** Eli enjoys looking at the sketch. Its disappearance causes Eli to press “clear” less often when a sketch becomes cluttered. That removal negatively punishes clearing the screen.

The button and its visible effect stay the same. Eli's response to the disappearance changes. “A sketch disappeared” supplies only half the answer; each version also tells you what that removal does to later clearing.

### The behavior decreases in both versions

Now imagine two versions of a racing game. In each, a consequence causes Ben to ram other boats less often.

- In one version, ramming adds an unwanted five-second vibration to Ben's controller: **positive punishment**.
- In the other, ramming removes a collectible flag Ben has already earned: **negative punishment**.

Both are punishment because ramming decreases. The added vibration versus the removed flag decides which kind. Calling both consequences “bad” would lose that distinction.

## One exchange can reinforce two different actions

Two adults share a worktable. Omar drums his fingers on it. Lena finds the tapping irritating and hands Omar a small puzzle to occupy his hands. Omar stops tapping while he works on it.

Suppose repeated exchanges establish both of these effects: receiving puzzles makes Omar tap more often in similar situations, and the end of the tapping makes Lena hand over puzzles more often when it starts.

| Whose action? | Consequence for that person | Later effect | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Omar's tapping | A puzzle is provided | Tapping increases | Positive reinforcement |
| Lena's handing over a puzzle | Irritating tapping stops | Handing over puzzles increases | Negative reinforcement |

The tapping stops during an individual exchange but becomes more likely across later exchanges. Those statements describe different timescales and can both be true. The immediate pause doesn't establish punishment of tapping.

If a question asks about Lena, keep tracking Lena. Omar's receipt of a puzzle doesn't make Lena's action positively reinforced. Her row follows what changes for her after she hands over the puzzle.

## Preventing something can reinforce behavior too

**Escape** ends an aversive event already happening; **avoidance** prevents it. Both can support negative reinforcement when the effect strengthens the relevant behavior. [BCcampus explains this escape–avoidance distinction](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/thescienceofhumanpotential/chapter/control-learning/).

Ana shaking sand out of her sandals illustrates escape. For avoidance, imagine Sara putting a cloth under a mixing bowl before stirring. The cloth prevents the irritating scraping sound the bowl otherwise makes against the counter. Preventing that sound makes Sara more likely to put down a cloth before mixing again.

The cloth is physically added to the counter, but the consequence specified as strengthening Sara's action is the **prevention of scraping**. This is negative reinforcement. Track the stimulus responsible for the behavior change rather than counting objects someone moved.

## A reward or penalty needs an effect

An organizer can call a consequence a reward without demonstrating reinforcement. A game can display “penalty” without demonstrating punishment. The classification depends on what the consequence does to the specified behavior.

Imagine an adult workshop offers a certificate after someone leads a discussion. If the account ends there, you know something was added. You don't know whether leading discussions becomes more likely, less likely, or stays unchanged.

Two other gaps are easy to overlook:

- **The outcome tracks another action.** A consequence follows volunteering, but the report only says attendance increased. That doesn't establish what happened to volunteering.
- **Several consequences change together.** If an action adds an obstacle and removes an earned item, then decreases because of that combination, the account doesn't isolate each component's effect. You can identify punishment overall without assigning one positive-or-negative label to the bundle.

A change over time also needs a causal explanation. If attendance rises after certificates are introduced and the workshop moves to a more convenient time, the timing alone won't tell you what caused the increase. Our practice scenarios supply the causal relationship so you can focus on classification.

## Try these ten operant conditioning practice questions

Cover the answers below. For each item, write the actor, action, consequence, and later effect. Give a classification only as specific as the account allows.

1. At a recipe exchange, contributing a recipe earns Inez a printed copy of the group's recipe collection. Receiving these collections causes Inez to contribute recipes more often. What process is shown?
2. A loose sleeve keeps brushing Theo's wet painting. Rolling it up stops the unwanted contact, and that relief makes Theo more likely to roll it up whenever it brushes a painting again. What process is shown?
3. In a virtual museum game, opening a sealed display removes a photograph from Mei's earned collection. Losing photographs causes Mei to open sealed displays less often. What process is shown?
4. Daniel tests whether varnish is dry by touching it. Touching wet varnish leaves an unpleasant sticky residue on his finger. Because of that residue, he tests varnish by touch less often on later projects. What process is shown?
5. A community newsletter prints Farah's name whenever she submits a puzzle. Nothing is reported about later submissions. Has positive reinforcement been established?
6. In a game, the designer adds a funny animation after a player deliberately drops a parcel, intending to discourage dropping. The animation instead causes Ruth to drop parcels more often. What process is shown?
7. Before an outdoor drawing session, Luis clips his paper to a board. This prevents the irritating flapping that otherwise occurs in the wind. Preventing it makes Luis more likely to use clips before later windy sessions. Is this punishment or reinforcement, and of which kind?
8. As a consequence of arriving late to a voluntary game session, Pavel loses access to a replay he enjoys. After repeated instances, his late arrivals become less frequent because of that loss. What process is shown?
9. A consequence of visiting a local exhibition makes Nora visit exhibitions more often. The description doesn't identify the consequence. How much can you classify?
10. Using a hint in a puzzle game adds a bothersome countdown and removes an earned illustration. The combined consequence causes Alice to use hints less often. Can you identify one positive-or-negative punishment category?

## Check your reasoning

1. **Positive reinforcement.** Inez's recipe contributions increase because a printed collection is provided. Contributing is the action; receiving the collection is its consequence.
2. **Negative reinforcement.** Theo's sleeve rolling increases because it ends unwanted contact with the painting. Negative describes removal of that contact. Punishment would require sleeve rolling to decrease.
3. **Negative punishment.** Opening sealed displays decreases because an earned photograph is removed. Both this item and question 2 involve removal, but their effects on the target behavior differ.
4. **Positive punishment.** Testing varnish by touch decreases because it adds sticky residue to Daniel's finger. No one needs to intend a punishment for the consequence to have that function.
5. **Not established.** Publication adds Farah's name to the newsletter, but the effect on future puzzle submissions is missing. A seemingly flattering consequence doesn't supply that evidence.
6. **Positive reinforcement.** Ruth's parcel dropping increases because the animation is added. The designer's intention doesn't change the demonstrated function.
7. **Negative reinforcement through avoidance.** Luis's use of clips increases because it prevents flapping. Adding a clip is part of his action; preventing the irritating movement is the consequence that reinforces it.
8. **Negative punishment.** Pavel's late arrivals decrease because access to an enjoyed replay is removed. Keep tracking late arrivals rather than silently switching to punctual arrivals.
9. **Reinforcement; positive versus negative is unknown.** Nora's exhibition visits increase because of the consequence. Without knowing whether it adds, removes, or prevents a stimulus, you can't classify it further.
10. **Punishment overall; the individual contributions are unresolved.** Alice's hint use decreases because of the combined consequence. The account doesn't establish whether the countdown, the lost illustration, or both would reduce hint use on their own.

## Keep the reasoning on your flashcards

For an item you missed, put the scenario and the exact action to classify on the front of a card. On the back, put the label and one sentence identifying both axes. An answer of “negative reinforcement” alone won't reveal whether you followed the right person or simply noticed something unpleasant.

You can also change one detail yourself: keep removal but reverse the later effect, or keep the decrease but replace removal with addition. Explain why the answer changes before checking the table again.
