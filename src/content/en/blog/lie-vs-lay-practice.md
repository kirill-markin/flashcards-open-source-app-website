“I lay the blanket on the bench” and “I lay on the blanket yesterday” both use **lay** correctly. The first describes placing something; the second describes resting somewhere. The spelling alone doesn't tell you the word's job.

For this lie vs lay practice, make two decisions: choose the meaning, then choose the form the sentence needs. The target is standard edited English. Other patterns occur in everyday speech; the [Merriam-Webster usage discussion](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/how-to-use-lay-and-lie) puts that variation in context.

## First, find what is being placed

**Lay** means to put something somewhere. In “I lay the blanket on the bench,” *the blanket* is the direct object: the thing I place. **Lie** describes resting or being in a position. In “The blanket lies on the bench,” the blanket is the subject. That sentence describes its position rather than someone placing it. [Cambridge's lie-or-lay guide](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/lie-or-lay) explains the distinction.

Compare the same blanket in two roles:

- “I **lay** the blanket across the bench.” The blanket is what I place.
- “The blanket **lies** across the bench.” The blanket is what is in that position.

A noun after the verb isn't automatically its direct object. In “I lie beside the blanket,” *the blanket* is the object of the preposition **beside**. The whole phrase tells you where I rest; it doesn't name something I place.

There is also a separate **lie** meaning to tell an untruth. Its past is **lied**. Decide whether the sentence concerns placing, resting or position, or an untruth before reaching for a verb form.

## Then check the form

| Meaning | Base | Past simple | Past participle | -ing form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Place something | lay | laid | laid | laying |
| Be or rest somewhere | lie | lay | lain | lying |
| Tell an untruth | lie | lied | lied | lying |

The [Purdue OWL irregular-verb table](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/irregular_verbs/index.html) confirms the base, past, and participle forms for the two irregular verbs: **lay–laid–laid** and **lie–lay–lain**. Notice the double job of **lay**: it is the placement verb's base form and the reclining verb's past form. **Lain** is the standard participle for position or reclining; Cambridge describes it as formal and rarely used.

The words around the gap tell you which column to use:

- With **did**, use the base form, even when the sentence includes *yesterday*. The [British Council's past-simple guide](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple) shows this question pattern.
- Perfect **have/has/had** takes a past participle: “The blanket has **lain** there all morning.”
- A passive also uses a participle: “The blanket was **laid** across the bench.” Someone placed it. The blanket is now the subject receiving the action, so a subject before the verb doesn't automatically mean you need *lie*.

The [past simple vs past participle guide](/blog/past-simple-vs-past-participle/) gives more practice with these sentence structures.

## Try these twelve sentences

Choose one answer for each sentence. Write down the meaning—**place**, **rest/position**, or **untruth**—and the clue that determines the form. Keep the answer key out of view until you've finished.

1. Every morning, I **(lie / lay)** the folded blanket across the bench.
2. Every morning, the folded blanket **(lies / lays)** across the bench until I put it away.
3. Yesterday, Ravi **(lay / laid)** down on the picnic rug to rest.
4. Yesterday, Ravi **(lay / laid)** his jacket on the picnic rug.
5. Did Elise **(lie / lay)** down after the hike?
6. We have **(laid / lain)** the spare keys beside the kettle.
7. The museum's original map has **(laid / lain)** in that drawer for decades.
8. Before the visitors arrived, the protective cloth was **(laid / lain)** over the display by a technician.
9. While we talk, the baby is **(laying / lying)** on the mat.
10. Please stop **(laying / lying)** clean towels on the wet floor.
11. Yesterday, I **(lay / lied)** about the missing receipt; I actually had it in my pocket.
12. Where did Nora **(lay / laid)** the folded blanket yesterday?

### Answers with the reason

1. **Lay.** I place *the folded blanket*, the direct object. The present simple uses the base form with *I*.
2. **Lies.** The blanket is in a position; the sentence doesn't describe anyone placing it. The singular subject takes **lies** in the present simple.
3. **Lay.** Ravi rested. *On the picnic rug* gives his location, and *rug* is the object of *on*, not of the verb. This affirmative past-simple statement needs **lay**, the past of reclining *lie*.
4. **Laid.** Ravi placed *his jacket*, the direct object. This affirmative past-simple statement needs **laid**, the past of placement *lay*. Questions 3 and 4 have the same time cue but describe different actions.
5. **Lie.** Elise rests, so start with reclining *lie*. The helping verb *did* requires its base form. Past time doesn't turn the answer into *lay* here.
6. **Laid.** We placed *the spare keys*. *Have* requires the placement verb's participle, **laid**.
7. **Lain.** The map has remained in a position. *In that drawer* gives its location, and *has* requires a participle: **lain**.
8. **Laid.** The technician placed the cloth. In the passive **was laid**, the cloth is the subject receiving that action. It doesn't need to appear after the verb as a direct object.
9. **Lying.** The baby is resting. *Is* plus the -ing form gives **is lying**: change *ie* in *lie* to *y* before adding *-ing*.
10. **Laying.** Someone is putting *clean towels*, the direct object, on the floor. After *stop* here, use the -ing form **laying**. Keep the *y* in *lay*.
11. **Lied.** The speaker told an untruth. That separate verb has the regular past **lied**; reclining *lay* cannot express this meaning.
12. **Lay.** Nora placed the blanket, so choose placement *lay*. *Did* requires its base form, even with *yesterday*. Compare this **lay** with the past-tense reclining **lay** in question 3: the spelling matches, but the jobs differ.

## Make a card for the mistake you made

Retry a missed question with the table visible. Name the meaning before completing the sentence. If that choice is wrong, practice what the sentence describes and which role each noun plays. If the meaning is right but the answer is still wrong, practice the form clue or spelling.

Use a fresh example for the repair card, so you have to apply the distinction again:

| Mistake | New card front | Back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treated a location as a direct object | Yesterday, Omar ___ beside the suitcase. Choose a form of lie or lay. | **lay** — Omar rested; *beside the suitcase* gives his location; past simple |
| Confused laid and lain after has | The parcel has ___ unopened for a week. (remain in position) | **lain** — position + *has* + participle |
| Put a past form after did | Did you ___ the tray here? (place) | **lay** — placement verb; *did* + base form |
| Assumed a subject before the verb must use lie | The tiles were ___ by the builder. (place) | **laid** — passive placement; the tiles receive the action |
| Confused laying and lying | The puppy is ___ beside the basket. (rest) | **lying** — reclining *lie* becomes *lying* |
| Used the reclining past for an untruth | She ___ about her age last year. (tell an untruth) | **lied** — regular past for this separate meaning |

Keep the sentence structure visible when it is part of the choice you need to practice. Say the completed sentence and explain the clue before checking the back.
