“When the builders arrived, Jo moved her bicycle.” In the usual reading, she moved it when they got there or just afterward. Change one verb phrase: “When the builders arrived, Jo **had moved** her bicycle.” Now she'd moved it before their arrival.

Choosing **past perfect or past simple** means choosing how to present the events. Work out what happened and when, then decide whether you're moving the story forward or looking back from a later past moment. An earlier event doesn't automatically need past perfect.

![A woman pauses at a snowy footbridge, looking at footprints that already cross it and continue along the path](/blog/past-perfect-vs-past-simple.png)

## Put the events on a timeline

Imagine these times on one morning:

| Time | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 9:00 | Jo moved her bicycle. |
| 9:10 | The builders arrived. |
| 9:20 | They unloaded their tools. |

Tell the events in order: “Jo **moved** her bicycle. The builders **arrived** ten minutes later and **unloaded** their tools.” Past simple carries the sequence, including the first event. [Oxford's tense comparison](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/grammar/online-grammar/practice/past-simple-and-past-perfect-simple_3) explains this use for successive actions.

Start instead at 9:10: “The builders **arrived** at 9:10. Jo **had moved** her bicycle, so they **walked** straight through the entrance.” *Had moved* looks back to 9:00; *walked* continues the story from the arrival.

That later past moment is your **reference point**: the moment you look back from. It can appear in a previous sentence, so past perfect doesn't require two verbs in the same sentence. The [British Council's grammar reference](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-perfect) explains looking back from a past time.

Compare the meanings before choosing a form:

| Sentence | Usual reading in this scene |
| --- | --- |
| When the builders arrived, Jo **moved** her bicycle. | She moved it at their arrival or just afterward. |
| When the builders arrived, Jo **had moved** her bicycle. | She moved it beforehand. |

With *when*, changing the tense can change the timeline. [Cambridge's comparison](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/past-perfect-simple) distinguishes events happening together or immediately in sequence from an earlier event marked by past perfect.

## “Before” and “after” can already show the order

Both versions work here:

- “After Lina **checked** the measurements, she cut the fabric.”
- “After Lina **had checked** the measurements, she cut the fabric.”

The checking comes first in both. *After* establishes that order; *had checked* explicitly looks back to the completed check. Likewise, “Lina checked the measurements before she cut the fabric” is grammatical without *had checked*. [Perfect English Grammar's lesson](https://www.perfect-english-grammar.com/past-perfect-use.html) explains that past perfect can be optional when *before* or *after* already makes the order clear.

*Because* doesn't choose the tense either. “The room looked larger because we removed the cupboard” can explain a clear cause. “The room looked larger because we **had removed** the cupboard” explicitly presents the removal as an earlier change. In its [reply about cause and event order](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-perfect#comment-213698), the LearnEnglish team explains that past simple is often possible when context or *because* makes the relationship clear; past perfect can remove ambiguity or emphasize the earlier action's influence.

There is a different use of *before*: “The screen went blank before I **had saved** my drawing.” Saving wasn't complete when the screen went blank. The sentence doesn't tell us whether the drawing was saved afterward. The [British Council's B1–B2 lesson](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/past-perfect) explains using *before + past perfect* for an action that wasn't done or was incomplete at the moment described in past simple. Read the relationship as a whole: *had* doesn't always label the event that happened first.

## Keep tense choice separate from verb form

Past perfect simple uses **had + past participle** for every subject:

- “She **had written** the address.”
- “She **hadn't written** the address.”
- “**Had** she **written** the address?”

*Had wrote* mixes the forms. Once you've chosen past perfect, use the participle. The [past simple vs past participle guide](/blog/past-simple-vs-past-participle/) helps with that part of the decision.

Past perfect can also describe a state lasting up to a past moment: “At the start of the tournament, I **had known** my teammate for three years.” Knowing that person may continue afterward; the tense doesn't say the relationship ended. Present perfect looks back from now instead: “I **have known** my teammate for three years.” For that time frame, use the [present perfect vs past simple lesson](/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/).

## Try nine past perfect vs past simple exercises

Use past simple or past perfect simple with the supplied verbs. Explain the timeline as well as giving the answer. Some questions accept both forms. These are original, imagined situations; preserve the meaning each prompt asks for.

1. The team finished putting up a barrier at 8:30. The inspector arrived at 9:00. Make the earlier completion explicit: “When the inspector arrived, the team ___ putting up the barrier.” (*finish*)
2. This time, the team finished putting up the barrier at the moment the inspector arrived. “When the inspector arrived, the team ___ putting up the barrier.” (*finish*)
3. Tell a camping story in event order: “Arun ___ the tent, ___ his bag inside, and then ___ dinner.” (*put up; carry; cook*)
4. “After Sara ___ the broken wheel, she tested the cart.” (*repair*) Is only one tense possible?
5. “We ___ the invitations before we announced the event online.” (*send*) Is *before* enough to make the order clear?
6. The cupboard was removed on Monday. On Tuesday, a visitor noticed the extra space: “The room looked larger because we ___ the cupboard.” (*remove*) Give a model answer and an acceptable alternative.
7. Give one standalone fact about an imagined town, with no later past reference point. Repair: “The town had built its first bridge in 1840.”
8. At her first chess tournament, Eva had no experience of timed games. Explicitly look back over her experience up to entering: “When Eva entered the tournament, she ___ a timed game.” (*never play*)
9. “The screen went blank before I had saved my drawing.” Was saving complete when the screen went blank? Does the sentence tell you whether the drawing was saved afterward?

### Answers and what they mean

1. **Had finished.** This marks completion before the inspector arrived. **Finished** would normally put completion at or around the arrival in this *when* sentence, so it wouldn't clearly express the earlier completion requested.
2. **Finished.** The two events coincide. **Had finished** would present completion as earlier and change the stated timeline.
3. **Put up; carried; cooked.** Past simple moves through the events in order. *Put* has the same spelling in its base and past forms.
4. **Repaired** or **had repaired**. Both place the repair before the test. *After* gives the sequence; past perfect also marks the look back to the repair.
5. **Sent** or **had sent**. Both preserve sending first. *Before* already tells us which action came earlier, so accept either with that explanation.
6. **Had removed** explicitly looks back to Monday's change from Tuesday's observation. **Removed** is also acceptable: the context and *because* make the relationship clear. A model answer can prefer past perfect without making past simple wrong.
7. **“The town built its first bridge in 1840.”** A distant date alone doesn't call for past perfect. Add a later reference and it fits: “By 1850, the town had built its first bridge.”
8. **Had never played.** This describes Eva's experience up to entering the tournament, as requested. **Never played** doesn't explicitly mark that earlier period in this sentence; it could instead describe what she didn't do during the tournament. The answer says nothing about her later experience.
9. **Saving wasn't complete then.** The screen went blank before saving was complete. Whether saving happened afterward is left open.

## Make review cards from the mistakes

Keep enough context on each card to judge the meaning. A bare “I ___ the door” doesn't tell you whether to narrate an event or look back from another one.

| Mistake to practice | Card front | Card back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lost the earlier event with when | The club voted at 6:00; Ren arrived at 6:15. Make the earlier vote explicit: “When Ren arrived, the club ___.” (*vote*) | **Had voted.** The vote happened before Ren arrived. |
| Added had to every story event | Tell these events in order: “Mina ___ the jar, ___ the lid, and then ___ it.” (*fill; close; label*) | **Filled; closed; labeled.** The sequence moves forward in past simple. |
| Rejected a valid alternative | “After Omar ___ the gate, he crossed the yard.” (*unlock*) Must the answer be past perfect? | **Unlocked** and **had unlocked** both work. *After* places unlocking first. |

For tense choice, leave the whole verb phrase blank. A prompt such as “Ren had ___” already chooses the tense and mainly tests the participle. Put accepted alternatives and their reasons on the back, so the card doesn't teach a false rule.

Try a fresh story before finishing. At 4:00, Imani put the picnic food indoors. At 4:10, rain started. At 4:15, everyone moved inside. Write one version in time order, then another starting with the rain and looking back to the food.

One chronological version is: “Imani **put** the food indoors. Ten minutes later, rain **started**, and everyone **moved** inside.” Starting at the later moment: “Rain **started** at 4:10. Imani **had put** the food indoors, so everyone **moved** inside to eat.” On your next review, change the events and explain the order again before choosing the verbs.
