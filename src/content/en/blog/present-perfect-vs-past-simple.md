“Nora repaired the bicycle yesterday. You can use it now.” The repair has a result in the present, but **repaired** is still the right tense: the sentence places the event yesterday. “Nora has repaired the bicycle” gives the news without placing the repair at a definite past time.

To choose **present perfect or past simple**, work out the speaker's time frame: a finished past occasion, or time leading up to now. Words such as *today*, *for*, and *already* are useful clues, but they can't make the decision on their own.

This lesson practices tense choice. Present perfect simple uses **have/has + past participle**, as in “has repaired.” If you've chosen that tense but aren't sure whether to write *took* or *taken*, the [past simple vs past participle guide](/blog/past-simple-vs-past-participle/) covers the verb form you need.

## Choose the time frame before the verb

Past simple presents events or states within a finished past time. Present perfect looks back from now: experience so far, a continuing state, or a past action presented through its current result. The action itself can be complete. “The team has painted the entrance” doesn't mean the team is still painting. The [British Council's present perfect reference](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-perfect) explains these uses.

| What the speaker wants to say | Example | Time frame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give an update on a task | The team **has painted** the entrance. It's ready for visitors. | Completed work presented as news now |
| Report when that work happened | The team **painted** the entrance last weekend. | A finished weekend |
| Count someone's experience so far | Leila **has judged** three competitions. | Experience up to now |
| Review a former role | During her year as club president, Leila **judged** three competitions. | Her presidency has ended |

Start by checking whether the event belongs to a definite past occasion. “Last weekend” makes that clear. A conversation can establish the occasion too: after “What happened at the workshop?”, “We painted a sign” needs no date. [Cambridge's tense comparison](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/past-simple-or-present-perfect.) explains definite past time and time up to now.

When no finished occasion is given, check what the speaker is doing: counting experience up to now, measuring a state that still holds, or reporting a current result. Present perfect often fits those meanings. The absence of a date alone doesn't require it; past simple also works when the past occasion is understood.

## An experience question can become a past story

Imagine two people talking about travel:

> “Have you ever taken a night train?”  
> “Yes, I have.”  
> “When did you take it?”  
> “I took one last winter. We left after dinner and arrived before sunrise.”

The first question covers the person's life so far. “When did you take it?” moves to a particular journey, and the reply tells its story. That question already asks about the past occasion, even before the answer gives a date. Cambridge describes moving from a general experience to specific past details in its [present perfect simple guide](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple-i-have-worked).

## “Today” doesn't choose the tense for you

Imagine a volunteer answering calls at a community center:

- At 2 p.m., with the desk still open: “I've answered twelve calls today.” The volunteer is counting from the start of the day up to now.
- At 7 p.m., after the desk has closed: “I answered twelve calls today.” The volunteer is reporting the completed shift.

The calendar day hasn't ended in either scene. The relevant working period has ended in the second one. Cambridge's [comparison of past simple and present perfect](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/past-simple-or-present-perfect.) shows that *today* can go with either tense, depending on how the speaker treats the relevant time.

“I've answered twelve calls today” is still possible after the desk closes: it can give the day's total up to now. Explain the speaker's focus when both choices work. An unfinished calendar day doesn't make every past-simple sentence with *today* wrong.

## Use “for” and “since” with the intended duration

**For** gives a length of time; **since** gives a starting point. Before choosing the tense, check whether the state continues:

| Situation | Sentence |
| --- | --- |
| The studio bought a printer in 2021 and still owns it | The studio **has owned** this printer **since 2021**. |
| It has belonged to the studio for five years and still does | The studio **has owned** this printer **for five years**. |
| A former owner kept it for five years, then sold it | The previous owner **owned** it **for five years** before selling it. |

Present perfect measures the studio's ownership up to now. Past simple describes the former owner's closed period. *For five years* works in both; it tells us the duration, not whether the ownership ended. The British Council compares [continuing and finished states](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect), and Cambridge explains [for and since with continuing situations](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple-i-have-worked).

Notice the two verbs in “I **have known** Rosa since we **met** in 2021.” Knowing her continues; meeting her was the starting event. A past date inside the *since* clause doesn't put the continuing state wholly in the past. The [British Council reference](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-perfect) shows this pattern. State verbs such as *own* and *know* keep these examples focused on present perfect simple rather than continuous forms.

## A present consequence can have a past date

“The shop **has replaced** the lock. The old key won't work.” This presents the replacement as a current update.

“The shop **replaced** the lock on Monday. The old key won't work.” The consequence is the same, but the replacement is placed on Monday. Keep past simple in the dated event clause. The [British Council's B1–B2 lesson](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect) distinguishes a present result from an event placed at a finished past time.

Keep the time expression attached to the right event. “I have used the new key since Monday” describes use extending up to now; it doesn't place one completed replacement event on Monday.

## British and American English allow different everyday choices

At work, you might hear “Have you sent the invitation yet?” In American English, “Did you send the invitation yet?” is also normal. Likewise, both “I've already sent it” and “I already sent it” can report completion.

Cambridge notes this greater use of past simple, especially with *already* and *yet*, in its [British and American English guide](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/british-and-american-english). Present perfect is also used in American English. Follow your course's expected variety, and distinguish a preferred exercise answer from a universal rule.

The same care matters with experience questions. “Did you ever take a night train?” can ask about life experience in American conversation. That wording alone doesn't prove the speaker means a closed period. To practice a closed period, supply one: “Did you take a night train during your trip last winter?”

## Try ten present perfect vs past simple exercises

Use the verb supplied. Complete or repair each sentence, then explain the time frame. Choose between present perfect simple and past simple. The model answers follow the usual British English teaching choices; the explanations accept other choices where appropriate. These practice situations are original.

1. **A closed course:** The course ended in June. Looking back, Ada says, “I ___ three essays during that course.” (*submit*)
2. **A course still running:** Ada is halfway through another course. She reports her total up to now: “I ___ three essays so far.” (*submit*)
3. **Life experience:** You don't have a particular trip in mind. “___ you ever ___ a night train?” (*take*)
4. **One trip:** The person answers yes. Ask for its date: “When ___ you ___ it?” (*take*)
5. **Still the owner:** Malik bought his camera five years ago and still owns it. “He ___ it for five years.” (*own*)
6. **Ownership ended:** Malik's sister kept an older camera for five years and then sold it. “She ___ it for five years before selling it.” (*own*)
7. **Repair the event clause; keep the date:** “I have paid the fee last Thursday, so I can join the class now.”
8. **Repair the continuing state:** Two friends met in 2021 and are still friends. “We know each other since 2021.”
9. **Explain rather than correct:** At 8 p.m., a volunteer says, “I answered twelve calls today.” The phone desk closed an hour ago. Must *answered* change to *have answered* because today isn't over?
10. **Recognize a dialect choice:** An American colleague asks, “Did you send the invitation yet?” Must the question change to “Have you sent the invitation yet?”

### Answers with reasons

1. **Submitted.** Ada is reviewing the completed course. Its whole period is in the past.
2. **Have submitted.** This is the best fit for a total from the start of the current course up to now. She may submit more essays later.
3. **Have you ever taken a night train?** This is the British teaching choice for experience up to now. **Did you ever take a night train?** can also be a life-experience question in American conversation; accept it when practicing that variety. A question explicitly limited to last winter's trip would use past simple.
4. **When did you take it?** The follow-up asks about the particular trip. *Take* follows *did*; the completed reply could be “I took it in February.”
5. **Has owned.** This measures the five-year state up to now. The context says Malik still owns the camera; “owned it for five years” would present the period as closed.
6. **Owned.** Her ownership ended when she sold the camera. The length of that closed period doesn't require present perfect.
7. **“I paid the fee last Thursday, so I can join the class now.”** Joining now is the consequence; paying last Thursday is the dated event.
8. **“We have known each other since 2021.”** Their acquaintance extends from 2021 to now. “We knew each other in 2021” is grammatical but changes the meaning: it describes a past state without measuring its continuation.
9. **No.** Past simple fits a report on the closed shift. “I've answered twelve calls today” is also possible as a total up to now; *today* alone doesn't settle the focus.
10. **No.** Both questions are accepted here. Past simple is normal American usage in this context; present perfect is natural in both varieties and is the usual British teaching choice.

## Make cards that preserve the reason for the choice

A card such as “I ___ a camera for five years” leaves out whether the ownership continues. Add that information before grading an answer. For correction cards, also say which meaning or detail must stay:

| Card front | Card back |
| --- | --- |
| Keep “last Tuesday.” Repair: “The café has changed its opening hours last Tuesday, so we can arrive later now.” | **The café changed its opening hours last Tuesday…** The change has a definite past time, even though the hours still apply. |
| Lin still owns her violin. Keep that meaning and “since 2020.” Repair: “Lin owned this violin since 2020.” | **Lin has owned this violin since 2020.** Ownership continues from 2020 to now. |
| A museum membership ended last year. Keep the completed period. Repair: “I have had that membership for two years before it expired.” | **I had that membership for two years before it expired.** The period has ended; *for* only tells its length. |
| A pottery course ended in July. Keep the question limited to that course. Repair: “Have you made a bowl during that course?” | **Did you make a bowl during that course?** The question asks about a completed period, rather than all pottery experience up to now. |

For tense-choice practice, leave the whole verb phrase blank or ask for a correction. “Lin has ___ this violin” already supplies the perfect structure and mostly tests the participle. The [cloze vs basic flashcards guide](/blog/cloze-deletion-vs-basic-flashcards/) helps choose the format; the [language-learning guide](/blog/how-to-use-flashcards-for-language-learning/) covers the wider review routine.

Try a fresh pair before finishing. A library's calendar records two completed events in the current month and four last month. More events are planned this month. Using *hold*, give its current month's total up to now, then report last month's total.

The models are “The library **has held** two events so far this month” and “The library **held** four events last month.” Explain which time frame reaches now. On a later review, change the situation and the verb so you have to choose the tense again.
