“Mina used to take the ferry” describes an old routine. “Mina is used to taking the ferry” describes something familiar. Add one small word, change the verb ending, and you've made a different claim about her life.

The choice between **used to, be used to, and get used to** starts with meaning: a former habit or state, familiarity, or becoming familiar. Then choose the verb form that fits.

## The same journey, three different claims

Imagine three possible accounts of Mina's commute. These are alternative stories, rather than three facts you must combine.

| Sentence | What the speaker tells us |
| --- | --- |
| Mina **used to take** the ferry to work. | Taking the ferry was her routine in the past; that routine has changed. |
| Mina **is used to taking** the ferry to work. | The crossing is familiar to her. |
| Mina **is getting used to taking** the ferry to work. | She's becoming familiar with the crossing. |

In the first story, Mina might now work inland. She could still take a ferry on holiday; it's the work routine that has changed. In the second, she knows where to queue and how boarding works. In the third, she might still check every announcement, although the journey feels less strange each day. Those extra details illustrate the meanings; the grammar alone doesn't supply them.

The [British Council's explanation of these three constructions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/different-uses-of-used-to) covers the underlying distinction. Here, we'll practice choosing which story a sentence tells.

## Choose the meaning before the ending

**Used to + base verb** describes a former repeated action or state: “This room used to be the ticket office.” A room's former purpose is a state; it doesn't need to be an action someone repeated.

**Be used to + noun, pronoun, or -ing form** describes familiarity: “Mina is used to the crossing,” “She's used to it,” or “She's used to standing on deck.” In this construction, *to* is a preposition, so a following verb takes *-ing*.

**Get used to + noun, pronoun, or -ing form** describes becoming familiar. “Mina is getting used to standing on deck” presents an ongoing change. “Mina got used to standing on deck during her first month” presents the adaptation as completed. *Get used to* doesn't always mean the process is still happening.

The practical distinction in **used to doing vs used to do** is therefore the whole phrase: “She **used to stand** on deck” versus “She **is used to standing** on deck.” If choosing verb forms is a separate difficulty, the [past simple vs past participle guide](/blog/past-simple-vs-past-participle/) practices looking at the surrounding structure.

### Familiar doesn't mean favorite

“Mina is used to crowded ferries, but she hates them” makes sense. Familiarity doesn't establish enjoyment, choice, or how often she travels now. An experienced traveler might take the ferry only occasionally.

To report a current routine, use a present-tense sentence such as “Mina **usually takes** the ferry on Mondays.” [Cambridge's guide to usually](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/usually) distinguishes typical behavior from former habits and familiarity. “She usually takes it” and “She's used to taking it” could both be true, but they answer different questions.

### Keep an eye on questions and negatives

For learner writing, use **did use to** and **didn't use to**:

- “Did Mina **use to take** the ferry?”
- “Mina didn't **use to take** the ferry.”

Keep *used* in the familiarity construction: “Is Mina **used to taking** the ferry?” and “Mina isn't **used to taking** it.”

[Cambridge's full used to reference](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/used-to) notes that spellings with *did used to* occur, while recommending the form without *d* for exams. Use that recommended form in the repairs below.

The *-ing* ending doesn't set the time. Compare “She **was used to working** alone” with “She **will get used to working** with a team.” The surrounding verbs establish the time. For other meaning changes involving these endings, try the [gerund vs infinitive practice](/blog/gerund-vs-infinitive-meaning-practice/).

## Try these used to and get used to exercises

Choose a form of **used to, be used to, or get used to**, unless other choices are given. Write the whole missing phrase and explain its meaning. Follow the requested focus; where two constructions are offered, explain the difference instead of guessing from a single clue word.

1. Mina traveled by ferry every weekday in her previous job. She now walks to an inland office. “Mina ___ the ferry to work.” Use **take** and describe her former routine.
2. Omar has just started commuting by ferry. Boarding feels less confusing each day, but it's still unfamiliar. “Omar ___ the ferry.” Use **take** and describe the change in progress.
3. Jo has crossed this bay for years. Crowded boats don't surprise her, although she dislikes them. “Jo ___ on crowded boats.” Use **travel** and describe familiarity. Does this mean she enjoys it?
4. For six months after moving, the ferry terminal announcements confused Ali. Eventually, they became familiar. Describe that completed change: “Ali eventually ___ the announcements.” Use **get used to**. Must you add an *-ing* verb?
5. The ferry terminal had no lift. There is one now. Complete the former-state sentence: “The terminal ___ a lift.” Use **not / use to / have**.
6. You want to ask whether ferries were Sara's regular transport before she moved. Repair this question for learner writing: “Did Sara used to taking the ferry?”
7. You want to say that the noise is still unfamiliar to you. Repair: “I'm not used to hear the engine.” How would “I didn't use to hear the engine” change the meaning?
8. Bea's current schedule is a Tuesday ferry and a bus on other days. Report that routine: “Bea ___ the ferry on Tuesdays.” Choose **usually takes / used to take / is used to taking**.
9. You start a job at the ferry terminal next month. You expect sharing an office to become familiar after some time: “I think I'll ___ an office.” Use **get used to / share**. Does the ending make this a past event?
10. “During his year working on the ferry, Dan **was used to sleeping / got used to sleeping** in a small cabin.” Both are grammatical. Explain what each version emphasizes. Does either sentence tell us his sleeping arrangements now?

## Answers and what they establish

1. **Used to take.** The ferry journey was Mina's past routine. The context explicitly supplies her present arrangement too.
2. **Is getting used to taking.** Omar is adapting. *Is used to taking* would present familiarity as already established, which isn't the requested focus.
3. **Is used to traveling.** This directly states Jo's familiarity with crowded crossings. Her dislike remains compatible with it. “Jo has got used to traveling on crowded boats” would also fit the situation, but would emphasize the change that brought her to this point.
4. **Got used to.** The completed sentence is “Ali eventually got used to the announcements.” The noun phrase *the announcements* can follow *to* directly; no extra verb is needed.
5. **Didn't use to have.** This describes a former state. Write *use* after *didn't* and keep *have* in its base form.
6. **Did Sara use to take the ferry?** The question asks about a past routine. “Was Sara used to taking the ferry?” would ask about familiarity at a past time.
7. **I'm not used to hearing the engine.** The sound is unfamiliar. “I didn't use to hear the engine” says hearing it wasn't a regular past experience; it doesn't directly describe your familiarity now.
8. **Usually takes.** This reports Bea's current routine. *Used to take* would describe a former routine. *Is used to taking* would describe familiarity, which the schedule alone doesn't establish.
9. **Get used to sharing.** “I'll get used to sharing an office” predicts adaptation. *Will* places it in the future; *sharing* is the form required after *get used to*.
10. **Was used to sleeping** presents an established familiarity during that period. **Got used to sleeping** presents the change through which sleeping in the cabin became familiar. Neither tells us where Dan sleeps now.
