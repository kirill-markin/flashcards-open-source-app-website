A card that plays a word while displaying `sheep.mp3` has already given you the answer. You might hear the vowel clearly, or you might read the filename and move on. The review can't tell you which happened.

**To make minimal-pair flashcards for listening, put one spoken word on the front and keep its identity hidden until you've answered.** Start with three pairs, check for visual clues, and keep unfamiliar recordings separate from the clips you review.

This worksheet helps you prepare cards and check them for clues. It supplies no audio or playable listening test. No recordings were made or auditioned for this article, and no learner trials or learning results are reported.

## Decide what your answer will show

A minimal pair contains two words that differ in one speech sound. That makes it useful for focusing on a contrast, as the [British Council's explanation](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/teaching-knowledge-database/d-h/minimal-pair) describes.

Choose one listening task before making cards:

| Front | Your response | What you're checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One spoken word, no written choices | Say or write the word you heard | Word identification without supplied choices |
| One spoken word, both choices visible | Choose between `ship` and `sheep` | Two-choice listening discrimination |
| A printed word | Say it aloud | Speech production from text |

Showing **both** choices is a valid listening task. Keep both equally visible on every card for that pair, without highlighting the target. Record the task as two-choice discrimination; it doesn't establish that you could identify the word without those choices.

A spoken answer can show which word you identified, but it doesn't by itself establish clear pronunciation. If you write answers, distinguish a spelling mistake from choosing the other word. For a broader mix of meaning, listening, and speaking cards, see [how to use flashcards for language learning](/blog/how-to-use-flashcards-for-language-learning/).

## Get usable recordings first

Use these three pairs for the first batch:

- **ship / sheep:** Cambridge gives /ʃɪp/ and /ʃiːp/ on its [ship](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/pronunciation/english/ship) and [sheep](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/pronunciation/english/sheep) pronunciation pages, with UK and US audio options.
- **sit / seat** and **fill / feel:** both appear in [EnglishClub's /ɪ/ and /iː/ lesson](https://www.englishclub.com/pronunciation/minimal-pairs-i-ee.php).

Listen to the vowel quality as well as its duration. EnglishClub describes a difference in mouth position too; simply stretching the vowel in *ship* isn't a complete pronunciation instruction. Use a consistent accent reference for this small batch rather than treating one voice as a model for all English speakers.

If you're studying alone without clips or a recording partner, begin with the [British Council's “Find your partner” audio lesson](https://africa.teachingenglish.org.uk/classroom/pronunciation/find-your-partner). It includes a listening model for *sheep / ship*. Use the Cambridge pages for individual word models. These are familiarization resources: their written labels reveal the words, so opening a named dictionary page isn't a hidden-answer test.

For your own cards, ask a teacher or fluent English partner to record the six words naturally, one word per clip, and allow you to use the recordings. Use copies you own or have permission to attach. The reference links here aren't permission to download and rehost their audio; keep those recordings on the original sites unless you have permission.

Have the speaker record both members of every pair using the same microphone and setup. If you use several speakers, each should record both words. A batch where one speaker always says /ɪ/ and another always says /iː/ lets voice alone identify the answer. Ask a teacher to check any recording whose intended word is unclear before using it as a prompt.

Make six cards, each with one target recording. This example shows how to arrange a *sheep* card; it isn't a listening trial. The brackets describe what you must supply.

**Front**

> Listen to the recording. Which word did you hear?
>
> [Audio control playing your authorized clip; no filename or transcript visible]
>
> ship · sheep

**Back**

> sheep — /ʃiːp/
>
> Replay: [the same authorized clip]
>
> Compare with ship — /ʃɪp/ — after answering.
>
> Private recording reference: M2; [your source and permission note]

For identification without choices, remove **both** words from the front and use “Say or write the word you hear.” Keep the answer format consistent within a check. Use a tool that supports audio and answer hiding, or have a partner play clips out of sight while you answer on paper. For the two-choice version on paper, the partner shows only the relevant pair, with both words equally visible.

## Look for clues before reviewing

Inspect the question side before playing anything. Can you work out the target from the screen?

| Possible clue | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| Filename, transcript, phonetic spelling, or target word in the title | Hide it on the question side; retain source details on the back. |
| A picture of a boat or sheep | Remove the picture from the listening prompt. |
| Only one choice shown or emphasized | Show both choices equally, or remove both. |
| Every other card has the same vowel | Shuffle the cards; don't rely on an alternating list. |
| One target has a beep, louder volume, or a distinctive background noise | Replace that recording and use the same recording setup for both words. |

Keep the natural sound of the vowels. Don't trim or stretch recordings to make the vowels artificially equal in length; fix unrelated recording clues instead.

Two common repairs are worth doing before adding more words. If you answered from a visible `sheep` label, hide the filename, title, and transcript, then inspect the front again. Repeat the card later in a new order. If you used the speaker's voice to choose, have that speaker record both words and rebuild the affected cards. Keep that batch within one accent reference while you repair the clue.

## Copy this blank eight-trial sheet

A partner can privately prepare eight presentations from the six recordings, allowing repeats, and shuffle their order. A repeated presentation plays the same file again; it doesn't require another recording. The partner keeps the presentation order and answer key private. Keep the player, ledger, and key out of the listener's view.

During ordinary card practice, answer first, reveal the word, then replay and compare. For a separate check with unfamiliar audio, ask your partner for fresh recordings of the same words. They keep the files and key private until the round, using another copy of the sheet. Don't add these clips to your review batch or listen to them beforehand. Repeats within that round still use the same files.

Count matches only among valid trials, and keep replay notes beside the results. If unused recordings are harder, inspect what changed: the voice, recording quality, or a familiar clue you had been using. This small round can't establish general listening ability or measure speech production. It can help you decide which pair or recording needs attention next.

Later, try another verified pair, such as *fit / feet* from the EnglishClub list. First make sure you know both words and their meanings. Keep that check separate from the fresh-recording check: changing the words gives you a different listening task.

Start by repairing one confusing pair. Add cards when they give you a new listening decision; our guide to [making better flashcards](/blog/how-to-make-better-flashcards/) covers keeping each prompt focused.
