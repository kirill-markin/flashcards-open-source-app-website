In เก, the vowel sign sits **before** ก on the page, but its sound comes **after** the consonant. Knowing the two shapes is only the first step. You also need to know what each sign is doing.

For Italian-speaking beginners, Thai alphabet flashcards are easier to use when each question has a clear target: a letter name, a sound, a class, or a vowel position. The tables and exercises below practise those tasks separately, then bring them together. They cover a selected foundation, with complete answers; full reading and tone rules need further study.

The companion [Thai consonants and core vowels deck](/it/catalog/packages/thai-alphabet-italian-speakers/) uses **Italian prompts and explanations**, with Thai as the language being learned. The Latin aids for letter names omit tones and do not reliably show vowel length. Use them to help remember a name, then check its pronunciation with a reliable audio model. This English guide is written for the same Italian-speaking audience.

## Ask one question about a letter at a time

A letter name, an initial sound, a final sound, and a class are different answers. For ก, keep the jobs separate:

| What you are recalling | Answer for ก |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional name | ก ไก่; the deck gives the rough Latin aid *ko kai* |
| Initial consonant sound | [k], unaspirated |
| Consonant class | Middle |
| Sound when used as a pronounced final consonant | [k̚], with no audible release |

Use the [NIU consonant materials](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/basic/alpha_cons_table.htm) to check letter identities and names, and its [middle-class table](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/basic/midclass.htm) for class membership. *Ko kai* is a memory aid for the name, not a phonetic transcription or the sound to insert into a syllable. Keep ก ไก่ as the written name you are learning. The final-stop notation is explained below.

A useful review prompt specifies the job: “Give the initial sound of ก.” If it only says “What is ก?”, you can remember the name while missing the sound and still feel that you got it right.

## Hear the breath in the initial consonant

![A learner watches a thin strip of tissue paper bend away from a small puff of breath](https://czaixyl0esghkd4s.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/marketing/870e9c21f8b1d6e5c724dbcdbc06956cb05da3b37ac4b620adea8dab371166ad.webp)

The small IPA symbol **ʰ** means aspiration: a puff of air after the consonant release. It does not add another syllable. These selected groups let you compare that feature while keeping voiced consonants separate:

| Voiced initial | Unaspirated initial | Aspirated initial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| บ [b] | ป [p] | ผ, พ [pʰ] |
| ด [d] | ต [t] | ถ, ท [tʰ] |
| — | ก [k] | ข, ค [kʰ] |

For an Italian speaker, ordinary Italian p, t, and the hard c in *casa* provide approximate starting points for the unaspirated column. Keep these sounds voiceless: removing aspiration does not turn [p] into [b], [t] into [d], or [k] into [g]. For the aspirated column, practise the extra breath after release. NIU’s [pronunciation guide](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/spokenthai/unit2/hints.htm) discusses the distinction for Italian learners and links listening examples. Written comparisons cannot check your production.

In these sound aids, **ph does not mean [f]**: it represents [pʰ]. Likewise, th represents [tʰ], not the English sound in *think*. The Latin letter-name aids also need care: their ch is not the Italian hard-c spelling. Read the Thai letter and check the sound requested by the question instead of sounding out the Latin aid as Italian.

Two more contrasts deserve their own practice. จ has the unaspirated initial [tɕ], while ฉ and ช have [tɕʰ]. ง represents the single nasal [ŋ], without a separate [g]. The [Thai phonology overview in Tantibundhit and colleagues’ paper](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS2011/OnlineProceedings/RegularSession/Tantibundhit/Tantibundhit.pdf), section 2, gives these initial sound distinctions.

Avoid reversing every card into “Which Thai letter makes [kʰ]?” Both ข and ค fit. If you want to practise producing one particular letter, add a constraint that makes the answer unique.

## Learn classes without turning them into tones

Use this selected class list alongside the sound work:

| Class | Letters practised here |
| --- | --- |
| Middle | ก จ ฎ ฏ ด ต บ ป อ |
| High | ข ฉ ถ ส ห |
| Low | ค ง ช น พ ม ร ว |

The middle-class row contains all nine middle-class consonants. The high and low rows are selections, checked against NIU’s [high-class table](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/basic/highclass.htm), [low-class table](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/basic/lowclass.htm), and [lesson 15 table](https://seasite.niu.edu/thai/maanii1/lesson15/structure.htm), which includes ว.

“High class” does not mean “pronounce this with a high tone.” Thai has five spoken tones, and the tone calculation involves the initial class, tone mark, syllable type, and vowel length. The [NIU tone chart](https://seasite.niu.edu/thai/selfassessment/module1/tone_chart.htm) shows that relationship. At this stage, learn the class label without guessing a complete syllable’s tone from it.

## A final consonant can have a different value

These values apply when the letter functions as a **pronounced final consonant**. They are not a rule that every written letter at the end of a word must be sounded.

| Letter | Selected final value |
| --- | --- |
| ก | [k̚] |
| ด | [t̚] |
| บ | [p̚] |
| ง | [ŋ] |
| น, ร, ล | [n] |
| ม | [m] |

The mark **̚** means no audible release: finish with the closure rather than adding a burst or another vowel. [Mok’s phonetic account of Thai final stops](https://ling.cuhk.edu.hk/people/peggy/ICPhS_07.pdf), page 2, describes that absence of release. NIU’s [lesson 11](https://seasite.niu.edu/thai/maanii1/lesson11/structureUni.htm) also distinguishes final p, t, and k from their written consonant labels. For ร and ล, the [DLTV lesson on final น sounds](https://dltv.ac.th/teachplan/episode/112661) groups both with น.

The useful comparison is positional: บ has initial [b] but final [p̚]. Remembering its initial sound alone does not answer a final-sound question.

## Locate the vowel before trying to read the whole syllable

The following combinations use ก to show a vowel spelling clearly. **They are sign illustrations, not a vocabulary list**: no word meanings are being assigned. IPA in this table gives only the vowel sound, without a tone or a complete syllable pronunciation.

| Illustration | Vowel sound | Vowel position relative to ก |
| --- | --- | --- |
| กะ | [a], short | After |
| กา | [aː], long | After |
| กิ | [i], short | Above |
| กี | [iː], long | Above |
| กุ | [u], short | Below |
| กู | [uː], long | Below |
| เก | [eː], long | Before |
| แก | [ɛː], long | Before |
| โก | [oː], long | Before |
| ไก | [aj] | Before |
| ใก | [aj] | Before |

NIU’s [vowel-position chart](https://seasite.niu.edu/Thai/SEAcamp/writingsystem.htm) shows the spatial groups; its [lesson 15 vowel table](https://seasite.niu.edu/thai/maanii1/lesson15/structure.htm) supplies the short/long contrasts and both ai spellings. In IPA, **ː** marks length. Hold the long vowel longer; do not treat กิ and กี as interchangeable spellings. ไ and ใ share the vowel sound shown here, but a word’s spelling determines which one to use.

For เก, find ก and identify เ to its left. Then put [k] before [eː] when arranging the sounds. You have answered two different questions: “Where is the vowel sign?” gives *before ก*; “Which sound comes first?” gives *[k]*. This is a sound-order exercise, without tone. Apply the same check to แก and โก.

These are the displayed forms only. Some vowel spellings change when a final consonant is added. Also, the short a in the shown กะ pattern is followed by a glottal closure; the table’s [a] isolates the vowel rather than claiming a complete pronunciation. NIU’s [syllable explanation](https://seasite.niu.edu/thai/maanii1/lesson11/structureUni.htm) covers that closure.

## Practise in layers, then mix the questions

Start with the names ก ไก่, ข ไข่, and ค ควาย. Recognise each letter shape and recall its name, then practise its initial sound as a separate question. Add the class labels and check that ข and ค can share [kʰ] while belonging to different classes. Keep any property you miss in the next review; knowing the name does not make a class answer correct.

Next compare an initial and final value, such as บ [b] versus [p̚]. For vowels, begin with กิ and กี: ask about position first, then sound and length. Once you can answer those separately, mix in กุ, กู, and the signs written before ก. This sequence is a way to organise practice, not a fixed daily quota or a mastery test.

Cover the tables for this short check. Write the requested property only, so that remembering a name cannot hide a missing sound.

1. Give the class and initial sound of ก as two separate answers.
2. ข and ค share which initial sound? Give each letter’s class.
3. In ป versus พ, what does the ʰ in the second sound represent?
4. Give บ’s initial value and its value as a pronounced final consonant.
5. When ร and ล function as pronounced finals, which sound do they share with น?
6. Where is the vowel in กุ? What changes in กู?
7. Which vowel is longer, the one in กิ or the one in กี?
8. In เก, which sound comes first, [k] or [eː], despite the written placement?
9. Give the vowel sounds in แก and โก.
10. Do ไก and ใก illustrate different vowel sounds? Can you freely exchange the two vowel spellings in words?

### Answers and the mistakes they catch

1. **Middle; [k], unaspirated.** Class and sound are separate properties.
2. **[kʰ]; ข is high class, ค is low class.** Sharing an initial sound does not require sharing a class.
3. **Aspiration, a puff of breath after release.** ป has [p]; พ has [pʰ]. This is not the voiced [b] of บ.
4. **Initial [b]; final [p̚].** The latter has no audible release.
5. **[n].** Do not carry their initial r or l value into this final role.
6. **Below ก; กู has long [uː] instead of short [u].** Both vowel signs are below the consonant.
7. **กี, with [iː].** กิ has short [i].
8. **[k] first, then [eː].** Written position and sound order differ here.
9. **แก: [ɛː]; โก: [oː].** Both vowels are long in the displayed forms.
10. **Both show [aj]; no free substitution.** Matching sound does not erase the spelling distinction.

Use errors to choose the next small review. A wrong class label calls for class practice; confusing กิ and กี calls for a length contrast. After checking a missed answer, try recalling it again after other questions, then return to it in a later session. If the written answers are right but your aspiration is uncertain, listen and imitate a reliable model: another written review cannot assess the sound you produced.

## Keep recall practice connected to listening and reading

The [Italian-language companion deck](/it/catalog/packages/thai-alphabet-italian-speakers/) separates names, selected classes, initials, finals, and vowel tasks. It does not contain audio or teach all vowel forms, consonant clusters, tone rules, dictation, or handwriting. Use the linked university pronunciation materials alongside it.

Try to recall the answer before revealing it, check the correction, and return to the material after a gap. [Retrieval experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/) support recall practice for delayed retention; they do not establish a guaranteed result for this Thai deck. [Spacing research](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CVRWP2008.html) also supports distributing learning over time, with useful gaps depending on how long you need to retain it. Keep the amount of new material small enough that you can still complete those reviews; there is no universal interval schedule to copy.

For the broader study workflow, see [how to use flashcards for language learning](/blog/how-to-use-flashcards-for-language-learning/). Then move from these controlled sign checks into guided syllable and word reading with audio. [Chulalongkorn’s reading and writing course outline](https://www.arts.chula.ac.th/CTFL/reading-and-writing-thai/) shows the wider progression: combining consonants, vowels, and tone marks comes before reading longer texts. Recognising the signs is a useful beginning, with more reading work still ahead.
