“On se voit à huit heures… enfin, à huit heures et demie.” The meeting is at half past eight. Here, *enfin* corrects the time; translating it as “finally” would send you in the wrong direction.

French conversational fillers are easier to learn when you ask what the speaker is doing: finding a word, correcting a detail, drawing a conclusion or inviting a reaction. Keep that job attached to a short exchange. An English gloss such as “well,” “so” or “you know” is only a starting point.

The examples below focus on conversational French as encountered in France, with English explanations. They aren't a register guide for every French-speaking region. The aim is to understand more of what you hear and choose a few expressions appropriately, without adding one to every sentence.

## Some “fillers” carry the connection

An *euh…* may give someone time to find an answer. A *donc* can connect a decision to its reason. A *bref* can compress several details into a summary. Treating all three as disposable noise loses useful information.

The Open University's [spoken French lesson](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/languages/intermediate-french-understanding-spoken-french/content-section-7) separates hesitations, fillers, repetitions and reformulations. For listening practice, that distinction helps you follow the message through an untidy sentence rather than expect every speaker to sound like a written paragraph.

| Job in this context | Expression | Original example | Meaning here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Take a moment to answer | *euh* | “Euh… je dois vérifier.” | “Um… I need to check.” |
| Lead into a response | *eh bien / ben* | “Ben… on peut essayer.” | “Well… we can try.” *Ben* is informal. |
| Correct a detail | *enfin* | “C'est au troisième… enfin, au quatrième étage.” | “It's on the third… I mean, fourth floor.” |
| Order two actions | *d'abord / ensuite* | “D'abord, on mesure. Ensuite, on découpe.” | “First, we measure. Next, we cut.” |
| Introduce a summary | *bref* | “Bref, il faut recommencer.” | “In short, we need to start again.” |
| React to information | *ah bon ?* | “Ah bon ? Je croyais que c'était demain.” | “Oh, really? I thought it was tomorrow.” |

These are selected uses, not permanent translations. In “Mon colis est enfin arrivé !” (“My parcel has finally arrived!”), *enfin* marks the end of a wait. The correction in the opening has a different job. The TLFi records both senses of [*enfin*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/enfin), as well as different reactions expressed by [*bon / ah bon*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/bon/adverbe).

OQLF's [list of connective functions](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/25159/la-redaction-et-la-communication/organisation-textuelle/liste-de-charnieres) groups sequencing and summary markers by what they do. It supports those functional distinctions, rather than a ranking of spoken frequency in France.

## En fait and au fait change different things

![A friend returns a borrowed umbrella as a man carrying a coat pauses at the doorway.](https://czaixyl0esghkd4s.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/marketing/e025e4b01c686b443fd716713e7b6dda376310f8bd49514009dfe2ee2d5e6147.webp)

The spelling is close. The conversational job can be quite different.

**Correcting an assumption:**

> — Tu travailles au bureau aujourd'hui ?  
> — En fait, je travaille chez moi.

“Are you working at the office today?” — “Actually, I'm working from home.” The reply replaces the assumed location with the real one. The Académie française explains [*en fait*](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/en-fait) through reality and appearances, and advises against turning it into an automatic substitute for *mais*.

**Bringing up a remembered question:**

> — Je vais chercher mon manteau.  
> — Au fait, tu as toujours mon parapluie ?

“I'm going to get my coat.” — “By the way, do you still have my umbrella?” Nobody is correcting the coat sentence. The second speaker remembers another question. This conversational [*au fait*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/fait/nom) can introduce something that has just come to mind.

For practice, label one cue **correct an assumption** and the other **raise a remembered question**. A bare “translate ‘actually’” card gives you much less help with the choice.

## Donc, du coup and a real consequence

Compare two original examples:

> “La bibliothèque ferme à dix-huit heures, donc partons maintenant.”  
> “The library closes at six, so let's leave now.”

The closing time supports the decision. *Donc* gives the listener the link.

> “Le verre s'est renversé. Du coup, la nappe est mouillée.”  
> “The glass tipped over. As a result, the tablecloth is wet.”

Here *du coup* connects an event to its immediate consequence. That is a useful starting context for learning the expression. The Académie's [careful-usage advice on *du coup*](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/du-coup-au-sens-de-de-ce-fait) emphasizes this temporal connection and objects to using it everywhere in place of *donc* or as an empty speech habit.

You may hear wider uses in conversation. A prescriptive recommendation doesn't describe everything people say. For your own careful explanation, state the relationship you mean: a consequence can take *donc*; a next step can take *ensuite*. A pause is enough when you aren't adding a relationship at all.

Contrast needs its own treatment. In “Ce vélo est cher. En revanche, il est facile à réparer” (“This bike is expensive. On the other hand, it's easy to repair”), repairability is a compensating advantage. *Par contre* can also fit that contrast. The TLFi entries for [*par contre*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/contre/pr%C3%A9position) and [*en revanche*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/revanche) support the overlap. Don't build a quiz that rejects one when the context allows both.

## Recognition comes before copying the habit

Register means how an expression fits the relationship and situation. Tone matters too. A phrase you understand in a friendly exchange may be an awkward default at a reception desk or in a prepared presentation.

| What you may hear | What to notice | A cautious choice for your own speech |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Il faut genre dix minutes.” | *Genre* introduces an informal estimate here. | “Il faut environ dix minutes” gives a neutral estimate. |
| “Hein ?” after a missed sentence | The speaker is asking for clarification or repetition. | Start with “Pardon ?” when speaking to an unfamiliar receptionist. |
| “On réserve maintenant, tu vois ? Comme ça, on a le choix.” | Here *tu vois ?* draws the listener into the explanation: “you see?” | Use “Vous voyez ?” with someone you address as *vous*, if the check is useful. |
| “Une table, des chaises, une étagère… des meubles, quoi.” | Final *quoi* rounds off the list: “furniture, basically.” It isn't a question meaning “what?” | Learn to recognize this informal use before deliberately adding it to your own sentences. |

The Académie discusses the relaxed approximating use of [*genre*](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/genre). The TLFi distinguishes uses of [*hein*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/hein), [*voir*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/voir/verbe) and final [*quoi*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/quoi/pronom). The cautious choices above are learner guidance, not claims that one expression is always rude or that professional speakers never use it.

Changing *tu* to *vous* doesn't fix an impatient tone. Nor does *ah bon ?* prove agreement: it can express surprise, disbelief or irony. In audio, listen to the delivery and what comes next. A printed dialogue can show a possible meaning, but it can't test whether you recognized that intonation.

## Choose the job, then the expression

Try these before reading the answers. Choose between the supplied options where given. The constraints make the task checkable; an open conversation could allow other wording.

1. **Correct an assumption:** “Tu penses que je suis médecin ? ___, je suis infirmier.” Choose *en fait / au fait*.
2. **Remember another question:** You agree on lunch, then remember a separate question: “— On déjeune à midi ? — D'accord. ___, à quelle heure ouvre la piscine ?” Choose *en fait / au fait*.
3. **Distinguish two senses:** Compare “Le rendez-vous est mardi… enfin, mercredi” with “J'ai enfin reçu la confirmation !” Which day is the appointment? What does *enfin* do in each sentence?
4. **Draw a consequence:** “Il n'y a plus de places, ___ nous choisissons une autre séance.” Choose *donc / pourtant*.
5. **Order the next action:** “D'abord, lave les légumes. ___, coupe-les.” Choose *ensuite / bref*.
6. **Make an estimate neutral:** Rewrite “La réparation coûte genre cinquante euros” using *environ*.
7. **Ask an unfamiliar receptionist to repeat:** Choose *Hein ? / Pardon ?* as your cautious default.
8. **Allow an overlap:** “Ce manteau est lourd. ___, il tient très chaud.” Could both *en revanche* and *par contre* fit?
9. **Distinguish a literal sense:** Someone points to a door and asks “Tu vois le numéro sur la porte ?” Is *tu vois* a conversational appeal or a question about seeing something?
10. **Remove unnecessary markers:** Your prepared presentation lists three unrelated facts, each introduced with *du coup*. Must each fact keep a connector? Explain your choice.

### Answers and reasons

1. **En fait.** “Actually, I'm a nurse.” It corrects the assumption about the speaker's profession.
2. **Au fait.** “By the way, what time does the pool open?” The speaker brings up a remembered question after agreeing on lunch. No claim is being corrected.
3. **Wednesday.** In the appointment sentence, *enfin* corrects Tuesday to Wednesday. In “J'ai enfin reçu la confirmation !” (“I've finally received the confirmation!”), it marks the end of a wait. One English gloss won't fit both.
4. **Donc.** “There are no seats left, so we're choosing another showing.” The second action follows from the first fact. *Pourtant* would signal a contrast with an expectation.
5. **Ensuite.** “Next, cut them.” It advances the sequence. *Bref* would announce a summary.
6. **La réparation coûte environ cinquante euros.** “The repair costs about fifty euros.” The estimate stays approximate; its wording becomes more neutral.
7. **Pardon ?** This is the safer initial choice in the stated situation. Recognizing *hein ?* doesn't require using it with everyone.
8. **Yes.** The warmth compensates for the weight, so *en revanche* fits; *par contre* can also mark the contrast. This sentence doesn't force a unique answer.
9. **A question about seeing the number.** The speaker wants to know whether the other person can see it: “Can you see the number on the door?” This is the ordinary visual sense of *voir*.
10. **No.** Keep a connector where it explains a real relationship. Separate unrelated facts with pauses or clear sentence boundaries; repeated *du coup* would suggest a consequence the cue hasn't supplied.

## Keep the context when you review

For each expression you miss, keep a short situation, the function and a register note together. Ask what the marker does before looking at the explanation. For production, constrain the task: “correct an assumption; choose *en fait / au fait*” is easier to grade honestly than “say something natural in French.” Our [language flashcard guide](/blog/how-to-use-flashcards-for-language-learning/) gives more examples of keeping usage in the prompt.
