The biceps brachii inserts on the radius, yet “the insertion always moves” is too strong a rule. The attachment has a conventional name. Which body segment moves depends on the task, including what is held still.

Learning **muscle origin vs insertion** involves two jobs: remembering the named attachments and explaining movement under stated conditions. The elbow examples and seven exercises below separate those jobs. They're intended for introductory anatomy and physiology students learning to read muscle tables.

## Learn the labels, then state the conditions

The familiar introductory definition describes an origin on a relatively fixed structure and an insertion on a structure drawn toward it. [OpenStax presents this fixed-bone and moving-bone case](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/11-1-interactions-of-skeletal-muscles-their-fascicle-arrangement-and-their-lever-systems).

A course table turns that description into named attachments:

| Term | How to read it |
| --- | --- |
| Origin | The conventional attachment described as relatively fixed in the reference movement |
| Insertion | The conventional attachment described as more mobile in that movement |
| Action | A movement the muscle can produce, stated with its joint or body region |

An action entry doesn't promise that every contraction produces that movement. Likewise, origin and insertion labels don't specify which part must stay still in every task.

For many limb muscles, the listed origin is proximal and the insertion distal. These words describe position along the limb; see [anatomical directional terms](https://nibomo.com/blog/anatomical-directional-terms/) if you need the reference.

When answering an attachment question, use the origin and insertion listed by your course or reference. When analyzing a movement, add what is stabilized and what is free to move. **Don't rename the attachments each time the task changes.**

## A hinge makes the assumption visible

Imagine two rigid strips, A and B, joined by a hinge. They form a right angle. An imaginary shortening connector spans the inside of that angle, attaching away from the hinge at **O on A** and **I on B**. For this thought experiment, O is the named origin and I is the named insertion.

Assume the strips can rotate in one plane, the connector draws its attachment points closer together, and nothing blocks that rotation.

1. **Hold A still; leave B free.** As the connector shortens, B rotates toward A. The named insertion I moves while O stays fixed.
2. **Reset the angle. Hold B still; leave A free.** Now A rotates toward B. The named origin O moves while I stays fixed.

The attachment points didn't move to new places on their strips. O remains on A; I remains on B. Only the constraint changed. In both versions, the angle between the strips decreases, even though a different strip travels through space.

This is a thought experiment, not an anatomical simulation or a mechanism we physically tested. It isolates one decision: identify the fixed segment before predicting the moving segment.

In anatomy, **reverse muscle action** describes cases where the conventional origin moves toward a stabilized insertion. [Anatomy educator Joe Muscolino discusses this distinction in ABMP's magazine](https://www.abmp.com/massage-and-bodywork-magazine/issues/mayjune-2023/whats-wrong-origin-insertion-terminology). Retain the conventional labels when reading your course table, and describe the changed stabilization explicitly. Reversing which segment moves doesn't necessarily reverse the joint action; the hinge angle decreases in both versions above.

## Two elbow examples with actual landmarks

Here, *arm* means the upper arm and *forearm* means the region between elbow and wrist. The humerus is the upper-arm bone; the radius and ulna are forearm bones. These examples focus on bony attachments and elbow actions, rather than complete attachment and action lists.

### Biceps brachii: scapula to radius

The two heads have separate origins on the **scapula**:

- The short head starts at the **tip of the coracoid process**.
- The long head starts at the **supraglenoid tubercle**.

The bony insertion is the **radial tuberosity of the radius**. These landmarks follow the [University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences upper-limb anatomy reference](https://medicine.uams.edu/neuroscience/education/medical-school-courses/human-structure-module/anatomy-tables/muscle-tables/muscles-of-the-upper-limb/).

With the shoulder region and upper arm stabilized and the forearm free, one familiar action is **flexion of the forearm at the elbow**. The [University of Washington's muscle anatomy notes](https://courses.washington.edu/pbio375/muscle/muscle-anatomy.html) list this action and emphasize naming the joint or body region.

Notice the bone: biceps inserts on the **radius**, even though its belly lies over the humerus. A muscle's visible location doesn't identify its insertion.

### Triceps brachii: three origins, one elbow insertion

The triceps has three heads:

- **Long head:** the infraglenoid tubercle of the scapula.
- **Lateral head:** the posterior humerus, proximal and lateral to the radial groove.
- **Medial head:** the posterior humerus, distal to the radial groove.

Their common tendon inserts on the **olecranon of the ulna**. With the upper arm stabilized and forearm free, the elbow action is **extension of the forearm**. [Tufts Medicine's scapular region and arm lesson](https://tuftsmedicine.pressbooks.pub/rwillson/chapter/scapular-region-and-arm/) describes these attachments and action.

“Scapula” alone doesn't distinguish the two muscles' long-head origins. Biceps uses the **supraglenoid** landmark; triceps uses the **infraglenoid** landmark. Keep the landmark attached to its bone in your answer.

## An attachment list isn't a complete motion forecast

A row headed “origin, insertion, action” compresses several kinds of information. Reading the first two columns doesn't remove the need for the third.

To reason about a proposed movement, identify:

- Which joint is involved and where the muscle's line of pull passes relative to it.
- The starting posture and which segments are stabilized.
- Whether the muscle is shortening, maintaining length, or lengthening under tension, and what other forces act.

An unspecified connection from bone A to bone B cannot tell you flexion versus extension. The path around the joint matters. Nor does an attachment list tell you whether the muscle's tension overcomes a load.

**Contraction doesn't guarantee visible movement.** A concentric contraction involves active shortening. An isometric contraction develops tension without an overall change in muscle length; in the elbow hold below, the joint angle stays constant. An eccentric contraction involves active lengthening, as when elbow flexors control a lowering load. [OpenStax explains these contraction types](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-4-nervous-system-control-of-muscle-tension).

If a question asks for a movement's plane and axis, first settle the joint and action. Then use the [planes of movement lesson](https://nibomo.com/blog/planes-of-movement/) to classify its path.

## Practice: explain the decision

Cover the answers. Treat each stated constraint as part of the question. These are simplified reasoning exercises, not instructions to perform the movements.

1. Complete the biceps bony attachment pair: origins on the `___`; insertion at the `___` of the `___`. Include both origin landmarks.
2. A student's note says, “Biceps inserts on the humerus because the muscle is on the upper arm.” Repair the note and explain the mistaken inference.
3. A reference lists an infraglenoid origin on the scapula and an olecranon insertion on the ulna. Which head and muscle does this describe? With the upper arm stabilized and forearm free, name its elbow action.
4. In the hinge model, B is now fixed, A is free, and the connector shortens. Which marked point moves? Should O and I exchange their names? What happens to the angle?
5. A person holds a load still with the shoulder and elbow positions unchanged. The elbow flexors produce tension isometrically. Must the named insertion move toward the origin during this interval?
6. A reference supplies only this information: muscle X originates on bone A and inserts on bone B. A student predicts that its contraction will flex the joint and move B. Does that prediction follow from the supplied information? Name two missing details.
7. During a controlled lowering task, the upper arm stays still and the elbow extends while the biceps remains active and lengthens. A learner concludes that biceps has become an elbow extensor. Explain the error.

### Answers with reasons

1. **Scapula; radial tuberosity; radius.** The short head begins at the coracoid process's tip and the long head at the supraglenoid tubercle, both on the scapula. Saying only “shoulder” loses the required landmarks.
2. **Biceps has its bony insertion at the radial tuberosity of the radius.** The note inferred an attachment from the belly's location. The tendon continues beyond the muscle belly to its attachment.
3. **The long head of triceps brachii; extension of the forearm at the elbow.** “Infraglenoid” distinguishes this origin from the biceps long head's supraglenoid origin. Naming the joint makes the action explicit.
4. **O moves; the names stay; the angle decreases.** O is attached to free strip A. I stays on fixed strip B. The model changes which strip moves, while retaining the same closing motion at the hinge.
5. **No.** The stated isometric hold produces tension without moving the load or changing the joint angle. Tension alone doesn't require the insertion to travel toward the origin.
6. **No.** The muscle's path relative to the joint is needed to assess flexion versus extension. Which segment is stabilized is needed to assess whether B moves. Loads and contraction behavior also matter; the attachment names don't supply these details.
7. **Biceps is resisting the extension, not producing it.** It still pulls in the direction of elbow flexion, while another force drives extension and the active muscle lengthens. The observed joint movement doesn't turn biceps into an elbow extensor or change its attachment labels.

## Keep the error on the card

For attachment recall, try: “Biceps long head: which landmark, on which bone?” Answer: “Supraglenoid tubercle of the scapula.” For a constraint error, use: “In the hinge model, B is fixed and A is free. Which named attachment moves?” Answer: “O, the origin; the labels stay attached to their original points.”

Keep those as separate prompts, on paper or as ordinary flashcards. One checks a landmark; the other checks whether you carried an unstated fixed-end assumption into the question.
