“The bowl was beside the vase, and it was cracked.” You can picture the arrangement, but you still don't know which object has the crack. Changing *it* to *the vase* makes the sentence clear. It also gives the wrong information if the writer meant the bowl.

Useful **pronoun reference practice** asks you to do two jobs: identify what the writer means, then make that meaning easy to recover. A grammatical repair that changes the facts hasn't finished the job.

## Find the meaning before editing the pronoun

A pronoun can refer to a person, thing, group, or idea. An **antecedent** is an expression that a pronoun refers back to. In “The seedlings are dry; they need water,” *the seedlings* is the antecedent of *they*.

[Purdue OWL's pronoun guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/pronouns/index.html) explains why clear reference matters. In ambiguous pronoun examples, two or more referents fit. A vague pronoun reference may leave the reader without enough information to identify a person or event. These situations call for different decisions:

| Situation | What the reader needs |
| --- | --- |
| Two plausible referents | Which person or thing the pronoun means |
| An unidentified actor | Who performed the action, if that identity matters |
| An unclear event reference | Which action, result, or combination of events the writer means |

In “At reception, they changed my booking,” *they* may mean staff. That can be enough in casual conversation; the unspecified actor isn't automatically a grammatical error. A complaint that needs to establish responsibility may require the name of a team or person.

A vague event reference might follow several actions: “The committee postponed the vote and cancelled the meeting. This upset the volunteers.” Did the postponement upset them, the cancellation, or both? Context may answer that question. If it doesn't, ask the writer before choosing.

Don't automatically assign a pronoun to the nearest noun. Word order offers clues, but meaning and context decide whether the reference works. And don't infer someone's pronouns from their name.

## Keep a two-meaning repair ledger

For an ambiguous sentence, write down two possible meanings before repairing either. Here's a complete record for the opening example:

| Record | Meaning A | Meaning B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original sentence | The bowl was beside the vase, and it was cracked. | The bowl was beside the vase, and it was cracked. |
| Intended referent of *it* | The bowl | The vase |
| Fact to preserve | The bowl was cracked; the bowl was beside the vase. | The vase was cracked; the bowl was beside the vase. |
| Clear repair | The bowl was beside the vase, and the bowl was cracked. | The bowl was beside the vase, and the vase was cracked. |

Each repair names its target and preserves the arrangement. Smoother versions are possible: “The cracked bowl was beside the vase” or “The bowl was beside the cracked vase.” Neither version tells us whether the other object was also cracked. Don't add *only* unless the context supports it.

Copy this blank record for another sentence:

| Field | Your notes |
| --- | --- |
| Original sentence and relevant context | … |
| Pronoun being checked | … |
| Possible meaning A | … |
| Possible meaning B, if there is one | … |
| Intended meaning established by context or the writer | … |
| Facts the repair must preserve | … |
| Clear repair | … |
| Read-back check: does the repair keep every relevant fact without adding one? | … |

If you can't fill in the intended-meaning row, keep both readings open.

## Some pronouns should stay

**Singular they is valid.** A sentence about one person can use *they* when the person's gender is unknown or irrelevant, or when those are the person's pronouns. Respect the pronouns the person uses. The verb still takes the form *they are*. The [APA guide to singular they](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/grammar/singular-they) explains this usage.

**Weather and time expressions can use empty it.** In “It was windy” and “It was almost midnight,” *it* doesn't identify an object mentioned earlier. [Cambridge's empty-subject guide](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/it-as-empty-subject) describes this grammatical role.

**This and that can refer to ideas or events.** [Cambridge's paragraph-reference guide](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/it-this-and-that-in-paragraphs) explains reference beyond individual nouns. “The ferry was cancelled. This left us on the island overnight” has an understandable event reference. Adding a noun, such as *this cancellation*, is one way to clarify a crowded passage; it isn't a requirement for every use of *this*.

Deciding whether a relative clause identifies a subset is a separate task, covered in [restrictive vs nonrestrictive clause practice](/blog/restrictive-vs-nonrestrictive-clauses/).

## Eight pronoun antecedent exercises

Treat each item as an independent context. Before editing, identify what the pronoun means or explain what information is missing. Then repair unclear reference while preserving the supplied facts, or explain why no change is needed. The answers below give acceptable repairs; other wording can work.

1. **Choose the supplied object.** The folder was damp; nothing is said about the notebook's condition. Repair: “The folder was on the notebook, and it was damp.”
2. **Identify responsibility.** The pool manager changed the opening time. A notice must name that person as responsible. Repair: “At the pool, they changed the opening time.”
3. **Name the intended event.** The rehearsal started late, and the doors remained locked. The writer wants to say that the doors remaining locked upset the visitors. Repair: “The rehearsal started late, and the doors remained locked. This upset the visitors.”
4. **Check an already clear reference.** Only the seedlings are being discussed: “The seedlings need water because they are drying out.” Does *they* need replacing?
5. **Keep singular they.** Morgan uses they/them pronouns. No other person is under discussion: “Morgan said they are available on Tuesday.” Is a pronoun repair necessary?
6. **Recognize empty it.** A diary entry begins: “It was nearly noon, and it was drizzling.” Must either *it* have a noun antecedent?
7. **Check an event reference.** The cancellation is the only event under discussion: “The ferry was cancelled. This left us on the island overnight.” Must *this* be replaced?
8. **Work without the missing context.** “The envelope was beside the drawer, but it was empty.” No further information is available. Give two possible meanings and explain what an editor should do.

### Explained answers

1. **The folder was on the notebook, and the folder was damp.** Naming the folder preserves the intended reference. “The damp folder was on the notebook” also works. Calling the notebook damp would add an unsupported claim and leave the folder's condition unstated.
2. **At the pool, the pool manager changed the opening time.** The context supplies the actor, so the repair doesn't guess. The original *they* can be understood as unspecified people, but it doesn't satisfy this notice's need to identify responsibility.
3. **The rehearsal started late, and the doors remained locked. The fact that the doors remained locked upset the visitors.** Both events remain, and the second sentence identifies the intended cause. “The late start upset the visitors” would change that cause. It might be true as well, but the context doesn't establish it.
4. **No change needed.** *They* clearly refers to the seedlings.
5. **No change needed.** *They* refers to Morgan, and *are* is the appropriate verb form. Replacing the pronoun on the assumption that singular reference is wrong would be an unnecessary edit.
6. **No.** The first *it* supplies the subject in a time expression; the second does so in a weather expression. Neither needs an earlier noun referent.
7. **No change required.** *This* points to the ferry's cancellation. “This cancellation left us on the island overnight” is also acceptable, but the original is clear in the supplied context.
8. **The sentence could mean that the envelope was empty or that the drawer was empty.** Ask which object the writer means. Conditional repairs are “The envelope was beside the drawer, but the envelope was empty” and “The envelope was beside the drawer, but the drawer was empty.” Neither repair establishes the other object's contents. Don't select one merely because *drawer* is closer to *it*.

## Put the intended meaning on the flashcard

A review prompt that says only “Fix *it*” can force you to guess the answer again. Keep enough context on the front to make the repair decidable. These three original cards stand alone:

| Front | Back |
| --- | --- |
| The tray was scratched; the counter's condition is unspecified. Repair without changing that fact: “The tray was on the counter, and it was scratched.” | **The scratched tray was on the counter.** The tray has the scratch; the counter's condition remains unspecified. |
| The library's booking team cancelled the appointment. An email must identify the responsible team. Repair: “They cancelled the appointment.” | **The library's booking team cancelled the appointment.** |
| Missing the train connection caused the passengers' extra expense. Clarify their account: “Our bus arrived late, and we missed our connecting train. This cost us extra.” | **Our bus arrived late, and we missed our connecting train. Missing the train connection cost us extra.** The bus arrived late, the passengers missed the train, and the intended cause of the expense is explicit. |

Save useful repairs in Nibomo or another review tool. [How to make better flashcards](/blog/how-to-make-better-flashcards/) covers keeping each prompt self-contained. After reviewing a card, try the same meaning check on a sentence from your own writing: name the intended referent, make the repair, then check that the facts survived.
