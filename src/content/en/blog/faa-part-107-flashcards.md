On October 27, 2026, the FAA’s Unmanned Aircraft General – Small test starts featuring questions with chart images embedded in the question instead of included in the test supplement. That change matters for anyone building **FAA Part 107 flashcards**: cards can keep airspace symbols, weather concepts, and operating rules retrievable, but they cannot teach you to interpret every unfamiliar chart extract you may see.

Build the deck from the FAA’s current Airman Certification Standards (ACS), then keep full charts and official sample questions open beside it. Cards handle small, repeatable knowledge. Chart work shows whether you can find and combine clues when the image is new.

## What changes on October 27, 2026

The FAA’s [April 2026 Airman Testing Community Advisory](https://www.faa.gov/training_testing/testing/April_2026_Edition.pdf) says that beginning October 27, the UAG test will feature questions with images that are not included in the test supplements. The advisory lists VFR and IFR Low Altitude charts as source materials for the graphics update. Its two published UAG examples use sectional-chart extracts.

An embedded image becomes part of the individual question. The advisory says the specific images used in active questions cannot be previewed because that would compromise exam integrity. It directs applicants to official sample questions to become familiar with the format.

That is the announced change. The advisory does not announce a new UAG blueprint, a different question count, a difficulty increase, or a new body of aeronautical knowledge. An older chart extract can still teach a stable symbol. It becomes weak preparation when you recognize only that exact crop.

If your appointment is before October 27, chart interpretation already matters. If it is on or after that date, practice finding information on chart sections you have not memorized. The skill is reading the chart, not remembering where one airport sat in a study supplement.

## Make sure you are preparing for the initial test

First-time pilots who do not qualify through the Part 61 certificate-holder route take the initial aeronautical knowledge exam, officially called **Unmanned Aircraft General – Small (UAG)**. The FAA’s [remote-pilot certification page](https://www.faa.gov/uas/commercial_operators/become_a_drone_pilot) lists the eligibility and application steps, from getting an FAA Tracking Number to scheduling at an approved testing center.

Current certificate holders have a different recency requirement. To operate under Part 107, they must have completed one of the applicable FAA online recurrent training courses within the previous 24 calendar months. That training is separate from the UAG initial knowledge test.

This distinction prevents a common material mix-up. A recurrent-training page can be useful for an existing remote pilot and still be the wrong main plan for a first-time UAG candidate. Label your deck `Part 107 initial`, record the date you checked each FAA source, and remove resources that never say which path they cover.

Part 61 pilot certificate holders with a current flight review also have an FAA online-training path for initial remote-pilot certification. Check the official route that applies to you before buying a course or copying someone else’s deck structure.

## Use the ACS as the boundary for your Part 107 flashcards

The FAA points candidates to three official study resources: the Remote Pilot Airman Certification Standards, the Remote Pilot Study Guide, and official sample questions. Its [Part 107 study-materials page](https://www.faa.gov/faq/where-can-i-find-study-materials-part-107-aeronautical-knowledge-test) keeps the current links together.

Start with the [Remote Pilot ACS](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/training_testing/testing/acs/uas_acs.pdf), FAA-S-ACS-10B. The linked revision is dated April 2021 and remains the version on the FAA’s current study-materials page as of this article’s publication. It organizes the knowledge standards into five areas:

1. Regulations
2. Airspace classification and operating requirements
3. Weather
4. Loading and performance
5. Operations

Those areas make a useful deck map. Use tags such as `acs:I-regulations`, `acs:II-airspace`, `acs:III-weather`, `acs:IV-loading`, and `acs:V-operations`. Add a narrower task or element code when it helps you trace the source.

The ACS sets the boundary; it is not a request to create a card for every line. Read the relevant FAA material, test yourself, and add a card when a small fact or distinction fails retrieval. A giant transcription of headings can feel complete while leaving chart reading untouched.

Date-sensitive rules deserve a source note. Regulations, FAA guidance, charts, and test materials can change. A card about an operating limit should identify the FAA source you checked rather than floating around the deck as anonymous trivia.

## Write regulation cards as triggers, rules, and exceptions

Regulation cards work when the prompt identifies a decision. A front that says `Part 107 operating rules` is too broad to grade. A copied paragraph may test reading endurance more than recall.

Use small trigger, rule, and exception prompts:

```text
Front: Who is responsible for making sure the sUAS is in a condition for safe operation?
Back: The remote pilot in command. The remote PIC must check the sUAS before each flight to determine that it is in a condition for safe operation.
```

```text
Front: May another person manipulate the flight controls?
Back: Yes, when that person is under the remote PIC's direct supervision and the remote PIC can immediately take direct control.
```

```text
Front: Does an airspace authorization waive another Part 107 operating rule?
Back: No. It addresses the specified airspace access; any separate rule still applies unless the FAA grants the relevant waiver.
```

This structure fits topics such as remote pilot responsibilities, visual line of sight, right-of-way, preflight action, registration, Remote ID, operations over people, night operations, waivers, and airspace authorization. Use the exact current FAA source to fill the backs.

Comparison cards are useful when two ideas keep colliding:

```text
Front: What is the practical difference between a waiver and an airspace authorization?
Back: An airspace authorization permits access to specified controlled airspace. A waiver allows an approved deviation from a specific Part 107 rule.
```

Avoid cards that reduce a rule to one number with no unit or condition.

## Learn airspace symbols, then read unfamiliar charts

Part 107 airspace cards should train a visual vocabulary: airspace classes, boundaries, airport data, tower and frequency information, special-use airspace, chart legends, and the cues that tell you authorization may be required.

Crop a small public FAA chart area when one card tests one symbol. Ask a specific question:

```text
Front: What does a dashed magenta boundary around an airport indicate?
Back: Class E airspace designated to the surface. A Part 107 operation within it requires prior ATC authorization.
```

Another card can isolate an airport-information detail:

```text
Front: Where would you look for the CTAF when a control tower is not operating?
Back: Read the airport data printed next to the airport symbol and identify the frequency marked for CTAF on the supplied chart.
```

Keep the complete chart outside the card deck. Once you know individual symbols, open a current sectional you have not studied and locate an airport, determine the surrounding airspace, inspect nearby restrictions, and find the relevant communication information.

The October update makes this division even more useful. Tiny image cards help you recognize ingredients. Full-chart sessions make you navigate scale, clutter, nearby boundaries, and several facts at once. Use the FAA’s published UAG examples to understand the presentation instead of assuming every image will resemble the same familiar sectional crop.

## Make weather cards that support decoding and decisions

Weather study can split into sources, coded reports and forecasts, and effects on small-aircraft performance. Cards are good for abbreviations and compact relationships. They are less useful when an entire METAR or TAF sits on the back as a paragraph you merely recognize.

Build in layers:

- decode one group, abbreviation, or time format
- distinguish an observation from a forecast
- connect wind, density altitude, stability, fronts, clouds, visibility, or precipitation to an operational consequence
- interpret a complete current report outside the deck

A narrow decoding card might look like this:

```text
Front: What does 121755Z mean in a METAR?
Back: The observation was issued on the 12th day of the month at 17:55 UTC.
```

A decision card asks for meaning:

```text
Front: How can high density altitude affect a small unmanned aircraft operation?
Back: High density altitude can reduce aircraft performance. The remote PIC should account for the conditions, loading, and the aircraft manufacturer’s performance information.
```

Do not memorize yesterday’s METAR as if it were a reusable answer. Pull fresh reports, decode them without notes, and compare your work with a reliable aviation-weather source. A real report forces you to handle order, missing groups, changing conditions, and the difference between decoding data and deciding whether an operation is safe.

## Cover loading, performance, and operations with scenarios

The last two ACS areas are smaller on a page than they feel during an applied question. Loading and performance includes factors that affect the aircraft and operation. Operations spans radio communication, airports, emergencies, aeronautical decision-making, physiology, maintenance, and inspection.

Use short cause-and-effect cards:

```text
Front: What can a center-of-gravity shift change in flight?
Back: It can change stability, controllability, and performance. The exact effect depends on the direction and size of the shift and the aircraft’s limits.
```

```text
Front: Why should loading and center-of-gravity limits be checked before flight?
Back: Operating outside the aircraft’s limits can reduce performance or controllability. Use the aircraft’s current loading and manufacturer information.
```

Work calculations and performance questions on paper. Write units, state assumptions, and interpret the result. A card can preserve a formula or relationship after you understand it. It should not become a shortcut around doing the calculation.

For aeronautical decision-making, keep some scenarios outside the deck. If the answer depends on weather, battery state, people nearby, airspace, and crew coordination at once, compressing it into a one-line slogan removes the judgment the question is trying to test.

## Turn official practice misses into small cards

Use FAA sample questions to learn the format and expose weak knowledge. Do not collect recalled live-test questions, exam dumps, or copied active content. The FAA keeps active questions confidential, and recognizing leaked wording is poor preparation for a chart image you have never seen.

After an authorized practice set, review every wrong answer and every guess:

1. Identify the ACS area and, when possible, the element behind the question.
2. Explain why your answer failed before looking for a card format.
3. Relearn the point from the FAA source.
4. Make one or two small cards only if a reusable fact, distinction, symbol, or procedure caused the miss.
5. Return to a different practice question to check application.

For example, a missed airspace question may reveal that you do not recognize a boundary symbol. That symbol can become a card. If you recognized every symbol but combined the chart details incorrectly, another isolated card may not help; schedule more chart interpretation.

The same process works for weather and regulations. ## Keep chart interpretation and full practice outside the deck

A strong Part 107 plan needs a clear “outside the deck” list:

- unfamiliar full sectional and chart extracts
- complete METAR and TAF interpretation
- multi-step calculations with units
- scenarios that combine weather, airspace, people, aircraft condition, and mission pressure
- mixed official practice questions
- timed full-format practice from a lawful, current source

Flashcards improve access to the pieces. They do not reproduce the search, selection, and judgment required when several pieces appear together. A card can ask what a magenta vignette means. A chart task makes you notice it, relate it to the airport and operation, and decide what else to verify.

Use a loop: practice in context, inspect the miss, store the smallest reusable lesson, review it over time, then return to context. If the same scenario keeps failing after its facts feel easy, spend less time adding cards and more time solving new scenarios.

## FAA Part 107 flashcards FAQ

### What should I study for the Part 107 initial knowledge test?

Use the current FAA Remote Pilot ACS as the scope, then study through the FAA’s linked Remote Pilot Study Guide and official sample questions. Cover regulations, airspace, weather, loading and performance, and operations. Check the FAA materials page again before your appointment.

### What exactly happens on October 27, 2026?

The UAG test begins featuring questions with embedded images that are not included in test supplements. The advisory lists VFR and IFR Low Altitude charts as sources for the graphics update, and its published UAG examples use sectional-chart extracts. It does not announce a new blueprint, question count, difficulty change, or knowledge-scope change.

### Should I memorize sectional chart screenshots?

Use small crops to learn symbols and airport information, then move to unfamiliar full charts. Memorizing one screenshot can create confidence tied to its layout. The test task is interpreting the image supplied with the question.

### Do certificate holders retake the UAG test every two years?

To operate under Part 107, certificate holders must have completed the applicable FAA online recurrent training within the previous 24 calendar months. That is different from preparing as a first-time pilot for the UAG initial knowledge test.

### Can flashcards replace Part 107 practice questions?

No. Cards are useful for rules, symbols, weather groups, formulas, distinctions, and lessons from misses. Practice questions and full chart work test whether you can choose and apply that knowledge in context.

## Finish with a chart, not another hundred cards

Useful **FAA Part 107 flashcards** make the small pieces easier to retrieve: what a symbol means, which rule applies, how a weather group decodes, or why a performance factor matters. Keep those cards mapped to the current ACS and verified against FAA sources.

Then close the deck and open an unfamiliar chart. Interpret it, answer current official sample questions, and turn only the repeatable gaps into new cards. That balance supports both recall and the chart-reading work expected when the new image presentation begins on October 27.
