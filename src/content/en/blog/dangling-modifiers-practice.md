“After measuring the doorway, Noor's sketch was revised.” Noor measured the doorway. Noor revised the sketch. But the sentence gives *Noor's sketch* the subject position after an opening that describes someone measuring. A sketch can't do that job, even with its owner's name attached.

In **dangling modifiers practice**, spotting the mismatch is only part of the job. A repair must preserve the story too. If a glass slipped from someone's hand, a revision that says they released it deliberately changes what happened.

The exercises below use the subject-matching convention for the introductory phrases shown here in edited academic English. Some sentences already work. Identify the opening, find the main grammatical subject, and check who did the action—or which person or thing the opening describes—before changing anything.

## Give the opening a subject that fits

A modifier adds information about something else in a sentence. A **dangling modifier** doesn't connect properly to its intended target. For the introductory phrases practiced here, that target should be the main clause's subject. [Purdue OWL's dangling-modifier guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/dangling_modifiers_and_how_to_correct_them.html) explains the subject test and two useful repairs: make the intended doer the main subject, or give the opening its own subject and verb.

Take the doorway example. The main clause is “Noor's sketch was revised.” Its subject is the whole noun phrase *Noor's sketch*, whose central noun is *sketch*. The possessive *Noor's* doesn't make Noor the subject. The [University of Toronto's guide](https://advice.writing.utoronto.ca/revising/dangling-modifiers/) also discusses this possessive trap and openings followed by empty *it*.

Here are two repairs, given the stated facts:

> After measuring the doorway, Noor revised the sketch.

> After Noor measured the doorway, the sketch was revised by Noor.

The first makes Noor the main subject. The second gives the opening its own subject, *Noor*, so the main clause can remain passive. Both retain the sequence and identify Noor as the reviser. Leaving out *by Noor* in the second version would lose that supplied fact, even though the modifier problem would be fixed.

## Ask who is described, not just who acts

An opening can describe a condition rather than an action someone performs:

> Covered in flour, the baker wiped the counter.

This works when the baker is covered in flour. Now change the context: the counter is covered in flour, and the baker wipes it.

> Covered in flour, the counter was wiped by the baker.

This works too. The opening describes the counter's condition, and *the counter* is the main subject. The counter doesn't have to do the wiping. In the main passive clause, it receives that action. The [MLA Style Center's modifier quiz](https://style.mla.org/quiz-dangling-modifiers/) includes a valid passive repair with this same subject-to-description relationship.

Passive voice alone doesn't make a modifier dangle. Check the relationship: *carrying a tray* needs a doer of the carrying; *covered in flour* needs a person or thing that is covered. For help forming the passive verb itself, use the separate [passive voice tense practice](/blog/passive-voice-tense-practice/).

An opening with its own subject needs a different check:

> After the volunteers had sorted the coats, the hall was locked.

*The volunteers* is already the subject of *had sorted*. The hall can be the subject of the main clause without being the coat-sorter. The sentence doesn't identify who locked it, and an editor shouldn't supply that identity without evidence.

## Keep a short record before repairing

Before rewriting an awkward opening, record its grammatical connection and the facts you need to retain.

| Check | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | Which words are you checking? Does the opening already have its own subject? |
| Main subject | What is the grammatical subject of the main clause? |
| Intended connection | Who performs the opening's action, or which person or thing does it describe? |
| Facts to preserve | Who did each action? When and why did it happen? Which actor is unknown? |
| Repair and read-back | Does the new sentence fix the connection while keeping the people, events, timing, and uncertainty? |

Knowing how to fix dangling modifiers doesn't require forcing every sentence into active voice. A full opening clause is often useful when different people do the two actions, or when the second actor is unknown.

Read the whole grammatical subject, rather than applying “pick the first noun” as a universal rule. These exercises concern introductory action and condition phrases, including a person-directed purpose phrase. They don't cover every kind of sentence opening. If the intended meaning isn't available, ask for it before choosing a repair—the same precaution used in [pronoun reference practice](/blog/pronoun-reference-practice/).

## Ten exercises: diagnose before rewriting

Treat each numbered item as a separate situation. Write down the opening, main subject, intended connection, and diagnosis. Then repair the sentence, or explain why it should stay. Keep the supplied facts, including uncertainty about an actor. More than one repair may work; compare meanings as well as grammar when you reach the answers below.

1. **Doorway and sketch.** Noor measured the doorway, then revised the sketch. Diagnose and repair: “After measuring the doorway, Noor's sketch was revised.”
2. **An accidental slip.** Theo was carrying a tray when a glass slipped from his hand. Diagnose and repair: “While carrying the tray, a glass slipped from Theo's hand.”
3. **Flour on the baker.** The baker was covered in flour and wiped the counter. Nothing is said about the counter's condition before wiping. Check: “Covered in flour, the baker wiped the counter.”
4. **Flour on the counter.** The counter was covered in flour, and the baker wiped it. Nothing is said about flour on the baker. Check: “Covered in flour, the counter was wiped by the baker.”
5. **A roof inspection.** The caretaker brought the ladder outside in order to inspect the roof. Diagnose and repair: “To inspect the roof, the ladder was brought outside by the caretaker.”
6. **Two events, an unknown locker.** The volunteers sorted the coats. Afterward, someone locked the hall, but the account doesn't identify that person. Check: “After the volunteers had sorted the coats, the hall was locked.”
7. **A form and an unknown sender.** Daniel signed a form. After that, someone emailed a copy to him; the sender is unknown. Diagnose and repair: “Having signed the form, a copy was emailed to Daniel.”
8. **A fountain in the square.** The speaker was crossing the square when a fountain caught their attention. Diagnose and repair the speaker's sentence: “Crossing the square, a fountain caught my attention.”
9. **A dim display.** Rafi checked the meter and could clearly see that its display was dim. Diagnose and repair: “After checking the meter, it was clear to Rafi that the display was dim.”
10. **Gloves before work.** Inez put on gloves before she opened the kiln. Check: “Before opening the kiln, Inez put on gloves.”

## Answers with the connection made explicit

### 1. Noor, rather than the sketch, measured

The opening is *after measuring the doorway*. The main subject is *Noor's sketch*, but Noor did the measuring. The phrase dangles. **“After measuring the doorway, Noor revised the sketch”** gives the opening the right subject and retains Noor as the reviser. The passive alternative shown earlier also works because the opening names its own subject.

### 2. Keep the glass's accidental movement

*While carrying the tray* describes Theo; the main subject is *a glass*. The glass didn't carry the tray, so the opening dangles. **“While Theo was carrying the tray, a glass slipped from his hand”** names Theo in the opening clause and keeps *a glass* as the main subject. The slip still happens during the carrying. “Theo deliberately released a glass” would change the accident into an intentional action.

### 3. The baker can stay as subject

The phrase *covered in flour* describes the baker, and *the baker* is the main subject. **No repair is needed:** “Covered in flour, the baker wiped the counter.” The context establishes the baker's condition. Moving *the counter* into subject position after the same opening would assign the flour to the counter instead.

### 4. The passive sentence also works

The opening is again *covered in flour*. This time its intended target is the counter, which is also the main subject. **No repair is needed:** “Covered in flour, the counter was wiped by the baker.” The counter has the described condition and receives the wiping; the baker performs the wiping. Those roles fit together.

### 5. Give the caretaker the inspection purpose

The opening *to inspect the roof* expresses the caretaker's purpose. The main subject is *the ladder*, which can't perform the inspection. For this person-directed opening, write: **“To inspect the roof, the caretaker brought the ladder outside.”** This retains who brought the ladder and why. The original's *by the caretaker* identifies the person bringing it, but doesn't make that person the grammatical subject. This diagnosis concerns the introductory purpose phrase here; it isn't a rule for every infinitive construction.

### 6. Leave the unknown locker unknown

The opening *after the volunteers had sorted the coats* is a clause with its own subject, *the volunteers*. The main subject is *the hall*. **No repair is needed:** “After the volunteers had sorted the coats, the hall was locked.” Sorting belongs to the volunteers; locking has an unspecified actor. “The volunteers locked the hall” would add an unsupported claim.

### 7. Name Daniel without inventing a sender

*Having signed the form* describes Daniel, but *a copy* is the main subject. The copy didn't sign, so the phrase dangles. **“After Daniel had signed the form, a copy was emailed to him”** gives the opening its own subject while retaining the passive main clause. Daniel remains the signer and recipient, signing still comes first, and the sender remains unknown. Making Daniel the subject of *emailed* would wrongly assign him that second action.

### 8. Put the speaker in the opening clause

*Crossing the square* describes the speaker, while *a fountain* is the main subject. The fountain wasn't crossing, so the phrase dangles. **“While I was crossing the square, a fountain caught my attention”** supplies the opening's subject and preserves what attracted the speaker's attention. “Crossing the square, I noticed a fountain” is another acceptable repair in this context.

### 9. Replace the empty main subject

*After checking the meter* describes Rafi. The main subject is empty *it*, which names no person doing the checking. The phrase dangles; mentioning Rafi later doesn't fix its attachment. **“After checking the meter, Rafi could clearly see that the display was dim”** makes Rafi the subject and retains both the dim display and Rafi's clear perception of it. This doesn't make empty *it* wrong in every construction.

### 10. Keep the sequence already expressed

*Before opening the kiln* describes Inez, and *Inez* is the main subject. **No repair is needed:** “Before opening the kiln, Inez put on gloves.” Inez performs both actions, and *before* says the gloves went on first. Changing it to *after* would preserve subject matching while reversing the sequence.

If a revision creates two complete clauses, check their punctuation separately with [comma splice vs fused sentence practice](/blog/comma-splice-vs-fused-sentence/). Correcting the modifier doesn't automatically settle how the clauses should join.

## Review a decision, then try a fresh sentence

Make a short recall card for a mistake you actually made. Include enough context to determine the answer:

| Front | Back |
| --- | --- |
| Eva checked a parcel. Afterward, someone sent her a receipt; the account doesn't identify who sent the receipt. Repair: “After checking the parcel, a receipt was sent to Eva.” | **After Eva checked the parcel, a receipt was sent to her.** Eva is the checker, checking comes first, and the receipt's sender stays unknown. |
| The parcel is soaked. Is this a dangler? “Soaked by the rain, the parcel was carried inside by Eva.” | **No.** The parcel is the main subject and the thing described as soaked. Eva does the carrying. |

Regular recall and spaced review can help you retain the diagnostic questions over time. You can save these prompts in Nibomo or another review tool, then continue with new full sentences. Remembering one correction doesn't establish that you can diagnose a different opening. On the next example, name the intended connection, produce a repair, and read it back for facts you accidentally added or lost.
